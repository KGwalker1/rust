## **1. Introduction and Background**

- **Louis Leon Thurstone (1887–1955)** was an American psychologist and a major contributor to psychometrics and the study of intelligence.
- He challenged Charles Spearman's idea that intelligence could largely be explained by a single general factor (**g**).
- Thurstone proposed that intelligence consists of several relatively independent mental abilities rather than one dominant general ability.
- His theory is known as the **Primary Mental Abilities (PMA) Theory of Intelligence**.

**Important publications:**

- Thurstone (1938): _Primary Mental Abilities_.
- Thurstone (1935): _The Vectors of Mind_.
- Thurstone (1947): _Multiple-Factor Analysis_.

## **2. Primary Mental Abilities (PMA) Theory**

Thurstone proposed that intelligence is composed of several primary mental abilities, each representing a relatively distinct area of intellectual functioning.

Instead of explaining intelligence through one general factor, he identified seven primary abilities.

### **The Seven Primary Mental Abilities**

**1**

**Verbal Comprehension (V)**

Ability to understand words, sentences and verbal information.

_Example:_ Understanding the meaning of a passage or interpreting a sentence.

**2**

**Word Fluency (W)**

Ability to rapidly produce words according to a particular requirement.

_Example:_ Producing as many words as possible beginning with a particular letter.

**3**

**Number (N)**

Ability to perform numerical calculations and solve arithmetic problems.

_Example:_ Performing calculations quickly and accurately.

**4**

**Space (S)**

Ability to perceive and manipulate spatial relationships and visualize objects in different positions.

_Example:_ Mentally rotating a three-dimensional object.

**5**

**Associative Memory (M)**

Ability to memorize and recall relationships between items or information.

_Example:_ Remembering paired words or associations between names and faces.

**6**

**Perceptual Speed (P)**

Ability to quickly identify similarities, differences and details in visual information.

_Example:_ Quickly identifying matching symbols or detecting differences between pictures.

**7**

**Reasoning (R)**

Ability to identify rules, recognize patterns, make logical inferences and solve problems.

_Example:_ Identifying the missing number in a numerical series.

**Memory aid:** V–W–N–S–M–P–R

| Ability              | Symbol | Main function               |
| -------------------- | ------ | --------------------------- |
| Verbal Comprehension | V      | Understanding language      |
| Word Fluency         | W      | Producing words             |
| Number               | N      | Numerical ability           |
| Space                | S      | Spatial visualization       |
| Associative Memory   | M      | Learning and recall         |
| Perceptual Speed     | P      | Rapid visual discrimination |
| Reasoning            | R      | Logical problem-solving     |

**Important distinction:** Verbal Comprehension and Word Fluency are different. Understanding words is not the same as producing words rapidly.

## **3. Theoretical Assumptions**

Thurstone's theory rests on several important assumptions:

1. **Multiple abilities:** Intelligence consists of several distinct mental abilities.
2. **Relative independence:** The primary abilities are relatively independent, rather than completely unrelated.
3. **Differential profiles:** Individuals may demonstrate different levels of strength across the seven abilities.
4. **Multiple-factor approach:** Intelligence should be understood through several factors rather than being reduced to one general factor.
5. **Factor analysis:** Statistical analysis of relationships among test scores can help identify underlying mental abilities.

For example, one individual may have high verbal comprehension and reasoning but relatively lower spatial ability. Another individual may demonstrate strong spatial and numerical abilities.

## **4. Factor Analysis and Thurstone's Contribution**

Thurstone made major contributions to the development of multiple-factor analysis.

### **A. Centroid Method**

- Thurstone developed and used the **centroid method of factor analysis**.
- It was used to identify common factors underlying correlations among psychological test scores.
- It helped develop the multiple-factor approach to intelligence.

### **B. Simple Structure**

One of Thurstone's important contributions was the principle of **simple structure**.

Simple structure means arranging factor loadings so that:

- Each test has a relatively high loading on one factor.
- Each test has relatively low loadings on unrelated factors.
- The factors become easier to interpret.

This makes it easier to identify distinct psychological abilities.

### **C. Multiple-Factor Analysis**

Unlike Spearman's emphasis on a general factor, Thurstone emphasized several group or primary factors that explain performance across different mental tests.

### **Important development: Thurstone's later position**

Initially, Thurstone challenged Spearman's general intelligence factor. However, later research using larger samples and hierarchical factor analysis indicated that the primary mental abilities were not completely independent.

A general factor could also be identified above the primary abilities.

Therefore, Thurstone's later findings did not support the idea that the seven abilities were entirely unrelated.

**NET trap:** Thurstone's theory is not evidence that general intelligence does not exist. His major contribution was demonstrating the importance of multiple mental abilities.

## **6. Thurstone's Intelligence Tests**

Thurstone and his collaborators developed tests to measure primary mental abilities.

Important examples include:

- **Primary Mental Abilities Test (PMA):** Designed to assess different primary mental abilities.
- **Differential Aptitude Tests (DAT):** Associated with George K. Bennett, Harold G. Seashore and Alexander G. Wesman; these are influenced by the broader multiple-aptitude tradition, but should not be attributed solely to Thurstone.

The PMA tests were designed to provide a profile of an individual's strengths and weaknesses across different mental abilities rather than relying exclusively on a single overall score.

## **7. Strengths of the Theory**

1. **Broader understanding of intelligence:** Recognizes that intelligence involves multiple abilities.
2. **Individual differences:** Explains why individuals may excel in different intellectual domains.
3. **Educational applications:** Helps identify specific strengths and weaknesses in students.
4. **Psychometric contribution:** Advanced the use of factor analysis in intelligence research.
5. **Foundation for later theories:** Contributed to the development of multidimensional and hierarchical models of intelligence.

## **8. Criticisms and Limitations**

1. **Intercorrelation among abilities:** The seven abilities are not completely independent.
2. **General intelligence:** Later research continued to identify a general factor underlying cognitive performance.
3. **Limited coverage:** The original seven abilities do not capture every possible aspect of human intelligence.
4. **Dependence on factor analysis:** The number and interpretation of factors can vary depending on the tests, samples and statistical methods used.
5. **Cultural and educational influences:** Performance on intelligence tests can be influenced by language, education and cultural experience.
