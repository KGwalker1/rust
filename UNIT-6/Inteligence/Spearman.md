# **Charles Spearman: Two-Factor Theory of Intelligence**

A complete, exam-oriented note for your **UGC-NET JRF Psychology (December 2026)** preparation, covering Spearman's Two-Factor Theory, general intelligence (g), specific intelligence (s), positive manifold, tetrad equation, intelligence measurement, contributions, criticisms and MCQ traps.

## **1. Introduction and Background**

- **Charles Edward Spearman (1863–1945)** was a British psychologist and statistician.
- He is regarded as one of the pioneers of psychometric intelligence research.
- He proposed the **Two-Factor Theory of Intelligence** in 1904.
- His theory suggested that every intellectual activity involves two factors:
  1. General intelligence (g-factor).
  2. Specific ability (s-factor).
- He used statistical techniques, particularly **factor analysis**, to investigate the structure of intelligence.

**Important publication:**

Spearman (1904), _"General Intelligence," Objectively Determined and Measured_.

Very important for NET

&#x20;Spearman is primarily associated with the **g-factor and Two-Factor Theory of Intelligence**.

## **2. Two-Factor Theory of Intelligence (1904)**

Spearman proposed that performance in any intellectual task is influenced by two types of factors:

1. General factor (g).
2. Specific factor (s).

According to him, every intellectual activity requires some degree of general intelligence, along with specific abilities relevant to the particular task.

The theory can be represented as:

\\[ A = g+s \\]

Where:

- \\(A\\) = Performance in a particular intellectual activity.
- \\(g\\) = General intelligence.
- \\(s\\) = Specific ability.

_Note:_ This is a simplified conceptual representation, not a literal universal scoring equation.

## **3. General Intelligence (g-factor)**

The general factor represents the common intellectual ability involved in performing different cognitive tasks.

### **Characteristics of g-factor**

1. **Universal:** It operates across different intellectual activities.
2. **General:** It contributes to performance in multiple cognitive domains.
3. **Relatively stable:** Spearman considered it relatively stable compared with specific abilities.
4. **Transferable:** It helps individuals perform successfully across different types of intellectual tasks.
5. **Individual differences:** Individuals differ in their levels of general intelligence.

### **Examples**

A person with high general intelligence may perform well in:

- Mathematical reasoning.
- Verbal comprehension.
- Logical problem-solving.
- Abstract reasoning.
- Learning new information.

**NET point:** The g-factor is common to all intellectual activities, although its contribution varies across tasks.

## **4. Specific Intelligence (s-factor)**

The specific factor refers to the ability required for performing a particular intellectual task.

Different activities require different specific abilities.

### **Characteristics of s-factor**

1. **Task-specific:** It is associated with a particular intellectual activity.
2. **Multiple:** Different activities involve different specific abilities.
3. **Limited transfer:** A specific ability may not necessarily contribute to performance in unrelated tasks.
4. **Individual variation:** People differ in their specific abilities.

### **Examples**

- Mathematical ability for solving complex equations.
- Musical ability for recognising musical patterns.
- Verbal ability for understanding language.
- Spatial ability for mentally manipulating objects.

### **g-factor vs. s-factor**

| General factor (g)                         | Specific factor (s)                         |
| ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------- |
| General intellectual ability               | Task-specific ability                       |
| Common to different cognitive tasks        | Different for different tasks               |
| Contributes across intellectual activities | Contributes to particular activities        |
| Relatively stable                          | Depends on the task and experience          |
| Explains common performance across tests   | Explains specific differences between tasks |

## **5. Spearman's Positive Manifold**

Important concept

Spearman observed that scores on different intelligence tests tend to be positively correlated.

This phenomenon is called the **positive manifold**.

### **Explanation**

- Individuals who perform well on one cognitive test tend to perform well on other cognitive tests.
- Individuals who perform poorly on one test may also perform relatively poorly on other tests.
- This positive relationship across different intellectual tasks suggests the presence of a common underlying factor.

### **Example**

A student who performs well in:

- Verbal reasoning,
- Mathematical reasoning,
- Memory,
- Abstract reasoning,

may possess a relatively high general intelligence factor.

**NET point:** The positive manifold provided an important empirical foundation for Spearman's g-factor theory.

## **6. Spearman's Tetrad Equation**

Spearman used correlations among different intelligence tests to investigate the existence of a general factor.

He proposed a statistical principle called the **Tetrad Difference**.

### **Tetrad Difference**

For four tests represented by A, B, C and D, the tetrad difference is:

\\[ r\_{AB}r\_{CD}-r\_{AC}r\_{BD}=0 \\]

Where:

- \\(r\_{AB}\\) = Correlation between tests A and B.
- \\(r\_{CD}\\) = Correlation between tests C and D.
- \\(r\_{AC}\\) = Correlation between tests A and C.
- \\(r\_{BD}\\) = Correlation between tests B and D.

### **Meaning**

- If the tetrad differences are zero, this supports a single common factor under Spearman's two-factor assumptions.
- Non-zero tetrad differences may suggest that additional common factors are required.

**NET trap:** Tetrad differences are associated with Spearman's Two-Factor Theory, not Guilford's Structure of Intellect model.

## **7. Spearman's Contributions to Intelligence Research**

### **A. Introduction of the g-factor**

Spearman introduced the idea of general intelligence as a common factor underlying performance across different intellectual activities.

### **B. Development of Factor Analysis**

His work helped establish the use of statistical factor analysis in investigating individual differences in intelligence.

### **C. Psychometric Measurement**

He contributed to the scientific measurement of intelligence through correlations among different cognitive tests.

### **D. Foundation for Later Intelligence Theories**

His work influenced several later psychologists, including:

- Raymond Cattell.
- John B. Carroll.
- Philip Vernon.
- Arthur Jensen.

## **8. Criticisms of Spearman's Theory**

Although influential, the Two-Factor Theory has several limitations.

### **1. Oversimplification**

- Intelligence involves multiple cognitive abilities.
- Reducing intellectual performance to only two factors may oversimplify its structure.

### **2. Neglect of Group Factors**

- People may demonstrate clusters of related abilities, such as verbal, numerical and spatial abilities.
- These group factors are not adequately represented in the original two-factor model.

### **3. Influence of Environment**

- Intelligence is influenced by education, experience, motivation and environmental conditions.
- Spearman's original theory did not fully explain these influences.

### **4. Limited Explanation of Creativity**

- General intelligence alone cannot explain all forms of creative achievement.
- Originality, motivation, expertise and divergent thinking also contribute to creativity.

### **5. Alternative Models**

Later researchers developed broader models that recognised multiple group factors or hierarchical levels of intelligence.

## **9. Comparison: Spearman vs. Thurstone vs. Cattell**

High-priority NET comparison

| Basis                   | Spearman                   | Thurstone                              | Cattell                             |
| ----------------------- | -------------------------- | -------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| Theory                  | Two-Factor Theory          | Primary Mental Abilities               | Fluid and Crystallised Intelligence |
| Main concept            | General intelligence (g)   | Multiple primary abilities             | Gf and Gc                           |
| Number of major factors | 2 in the original theory   | 7 primary abilities                    | 2 broad abilities                   |
| Nature                  | General + specific         | Multiple relatively distinct abilities | Fluid + crystallised abilities      |
| Important year          | 1904                       | 1938                                   | 1941                                |
| Main emphasis           | Common intellectual factor | Independent mental abilities           | Reasoning vs. acquired knowledge    |

### **Important distinction**

- **Spearman:** One general factor plus specific factors.
- **Thurstone:** Seven Primary Mental Abilities.
- **Cattell:** Fluid Intelligence (Gf) and Crystallised Intelligence (Gc).
