# **Relationship Between Intelligence and Creativity**

A complete, exam-oriented note for your **UGC-NET JRF Psychology (December 2026)** preparation, covering definitions, major theories, the relationship between intelligence and creativity, threshold hypothesis, important researchers, empirical findings, comparisons and MCQ traps.

## **1. Introduction**

Intelligence and creativity are two important aspects of human cognitive functioning. Although they are related, they are not identical.

- **Intelligence** generally refers to the ability to learn, reason, understand, solve problems and adapt to new situations.
- **Creativity** refers to the ability to produce ideas, solutions or products that are both novel and appropriate or useful.

The relationship between intelligence and creativity has been extensively investigated in psychology.

The central question is:

**Does a highly intelligent person necessarily possess high creativity?**

Research suggests that intelligence contributes to creative thinking, but high intelligence alone does not guarantee creativity.

## **2. Major Views on the Relationship Between Intelligence and Creativity**

Psychologists have proposed different explanations for the relationship.

### **A. Creativity and Intelligence Are the Same**

According to this perspective, creativity is an aspect of general intellectual functioning.

- Creative thinking requires cognitive abilities such as reasoning, memory and problem-solving.
- Individuals with higher intelligence may possess greater cognitive resources for creative activities.
- Creativity can therefore be viewed as an advanced expression of intelligence.

**Limitation:** This perspective does not adequately explain why some individuals with high intelligence demonstrate relatively low creativity.

### **B. Creativity and Intelligence Are Independent**

According to this perspective, intelligence and creativity represent different cognitive abilities.

- Intelligence involves logical reasoning and problem-solving.
- Creativity involves generating novel and original ideas.
- A person may possess high intelligence without being highly creative.
- Similarly, an individual may demonstrate high creativity without having exceptionally high IQ scores.

**Associated researchers:**

- Getzels and Jackson (1962).
- Wallach and Kogan (1965).

### **C. Creativity and Intelligence Are Related but Distinct**

This is a more balanced perspective.

- Intelligence provides cognitive resources necessary for creative thinking.
- Creativity involves additional abilities such as originality, divergent thinking and flexibility.
- Intelligence and creativity overlap, but neither completely determines the other.

This view is consistent with much contemporary research.

## **3. Guilford's Contribution**

Important for NET

J. P. Guilford highlighted the relationship between creativity and intelligence through his **Structure of Intellect (SOI) model**.

He distinguished between two major types of intellectual production.

### **A. Convergent Thinking**

- Involves narrowing down different possibilities to arrive at one appropriate answer.
- Associated with logical reasoning and structured problem-solving.
- Commonly assessed in conventional intelligence tests.

Example: Solving a mathematical problem with one correct answer.

### **B. Divergent Thinking**

- Involves generating multiple possible responses to a problem.
- Encourages originality, flexibility and fluency.
- Considered an important component of creative thinking.

Example: Generating as many unusual uses as possible for a newspaper.

| Convergent Thinking           | Divergent Thinking          |
| ----------------------------- | --------------------------- |
| One best answer               | Multiple possible answers   |
| Narrowing possibilities       | Expanding possibilities     |
| Logical selection             | Idea generation             |
| Accuracy                      | Fluency and originality     |
| Intelligence-test association | Creativity-test association |

**Important:** Guilford did not argue that intelligence consists only of convergent thinking. Divergent production is itself an intellectual operation in his SOI model.

## **4. Getzels and Jackson (1962)**

Getzels and Jackson investigated the relationship between creativity and intelligence among gifted schoolchildren.

### **Main findings**

- High intelligence does not necessarily indicate high creativity.
- Some students with high creativity scores had lower IQ scores than students in the high-IQ group.
- High-creativity students could demonstrate academic achievement comparable to high-IQ students in their study.
- Creativity should be assessed separately from conventional intelligence.

**Major work:** _Creativity and Intelligence: Explorations with Gifted Students_ (1962).

**NET point:** Their research challenged the assumption that the most intelligent students are necessarily the most creative.

## **5. Wallach and Kogan (1965)**

Wallach and Kogan further examined the creativity–intelligence relationship.

They emphasised that creativity assessment should occur under relaxed and non-evaluative conditions.

### **Major contributions**

- Creativity and intelligence are distinguishable abilities.
- Creativity involves generating numerous and unique associations.
- Testing conditions can influence creative performance.
- Strict time limits and evaluative pressure may restrict the expression of creative potential.

### **Two major dimensions**

| Dimension    | Meaning                            |
| ------------ | ---------------------------------- |
| Productivity | Number of responses generated      |
| Uniqueness   | Unusualness or rarity of responses |

**NET point:** Wallach and Kogan are particularly associated with the importance of a playful, relaxed and untimed environment for creativity assessment.

## **6. Threshold Hypothesis**

Very important for NET

The **Threshold Hypothesis** proposes that intelligence and creativity are positively related up to a certain level of intelligence, beyond which the relationship becomes weaker or less significant.

It suggests that a minimum level of intelligence may be necessary for creative performance, but exceptionally high intelligence does not necessarily produce exceptionally high creativity.

The commonly cited threshold is approximately **IQ 120**.

### **Explanation**

**Below IQ 120:**

- Intelligence and creativity tend to show a positive relationship.
- Greater intellectual ability may facilitate creative thinking.

**Above IQ 120:**

- The relationship becomes weaker.
- Other factors become increasingly important in explaining creativity.
- Motivation, personality, knowledge, environment and opportunities may contribute substantially.

**Conceptual illustration of the Threshold Hypothesis**

Schematic illustration, not actual empirical data. The threshold is an approximate value, not a universal IQ cutoff.

### **Researchers associated with the Threshold Hypothesis**

- **Donald W. MacKinnon** – Research on creativity and highly creative individuals.
- **Calvin W. Taylor** – Research on giftedness and creativity.
- **Paul E. Vernon** – Discussed the relationship between intelligence and creativity.

**Important qualification:** The IQ 120 threshold is a historically influential proposal, not a universally established scientific boundary. Later research has found that the relationship varies according to the creativity measure, population and context.

## **7. Sternberg and Lubart: Investment Theory of Creativity**

Robert J. Sternberg and Todd I. Lubart proposed the **Investment Theory of Creativity**.

According to this theory, creativity results from the interaction of several important resources.

The six resources are:

1. Intellectual abilities.
2. Knowledge.
3. Thinking styles.
4. Personality.
5. Motivation.
6. Environmental context.

### **Role of intelligence**

Sternberg and Lubart identified three important intellectual abilities:

| Ability           | Meaning                                                |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| Synthetic ability | Generating novel ideas and seeing problems in new ways |
| Analytic ability  | Evaluating and judging ideas                           |
| Practical ability | Implementing and communicating ideas effectively       |

**Central idea:** Intelligence is necessary for creativity, but it is not sufficient by itself.

## **8. Amabile's Componential Theory of Creativity**

**Teresa Amabile (1983)** proposed the Componential Theory of Creativity.

According to her theory, creativity depends on the interaction of three major components.

1. Domain-relevant skills.
2. Creativity-relevant processes.
3. Intrinsic task motivation.

Later developments of the model also incorporated the social environment.

### **Explanation**

| Component                     | Meaning                                                                 |
| ----------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Domain-relevant skills        | Knowledge, technical skills and expertise in a particular field         |
| Creativity-relevant processes | Flexible thinking, independence and creative problem-solving strategies |
| Intrinsic task motivation     | Interest, enjoyment and internal motivation to perform the task         |

**NET point:** Amabile emphasised that intrinsic motivation is particularly important for creative performance.

## **10. Factors Influencing the Relationship Between Intelligence and Creativity**

Intelligence is only one factor involved in creative performance.

Other important influences include:

**1. Knowledge and expertise**

- Creative thinking requires sufficient knowledge of a particular field.
- Expertise helps individuals identify problems and develop meaningful solutions.

**2. Motivation**

- Intrinsic motivation encourages exploration and persistence.
- Highly intelligent individuals may not necessarily be motivated to engage in creative activities.

**3. Personality**

Certain personality characteristics can support creativity:

- Openness to experience.
- Independence.
- Curiosity.
- Tolerance of ambiguity.
- Willingness to take intellectual risks.

**4. Environment**

- Freedom of expression.
- Supportive teachers and parents.
- Availability of resources.
- Acceptance of unconventional ideas.

**5. Cognitive flexibility**

- Ability to change perspectives.
- Ability to approach problems differently.
- Ability to generate alternative solutions.

## **12. Common UGC-NET MCQ Traps**

1\. **Does high intelligence always guarantee high creativity?**

Answer: No.

2\. **Who emphasised divergent thinking as an important component of creativity?**

Answer: J. P. Guilford.

3\. **Who studied the creativity–intelligence relationship among gifted schoolchildren in 1962?**

Answer: Getzels and Jackson.

4\. **Who emphasised relaxed and non-evaluative conditions for creativity assessment?**

Answer: Wallach and Kogan.

5\. **What is the approximate IQ threshold commonly associated with the Threshold Hypothesis?**

Answer: 120.

6\. **Which theory proposes six resources necessary for creativity?**

Answer: Sternberg and Lubart's Investment Theory.

7\. **Who proposed the Componential Theory of Creativity?**

Answer: Teresa Amabile.

8\. **Which three components are central to Amabile's original model?**

Answer: Domain-relevant skills, creativity-relevant processes and intrinsic task motivation.

9\. **Which type of thinking involves generating multiple possible answers?**

Answer: Divergent thinking.

10\. **Which type of thinking involves narrowing down possibilities to one appropriate answer?**

Answer: Convergent thinking.
