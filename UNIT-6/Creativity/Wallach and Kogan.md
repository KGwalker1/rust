## **1. Introduction and Background**

- **Michael A. Wallach** and **Nathan Kogan** were psychologists known for their research on creativity and intelligence.
- They investigated whether creativity represents a distinct cognitive ability from conventional intelligence.
- Their research challenged traditional methods of creativity assessment, particularly the use of highly structured, timed and evaluative testing conditions.
- They emphasised the importance of a **relaxed, playful and non-evaluative environment** for assessing creative thinking.

**Important work:**

_Modes of Thinking in Young Children: A Study of the Creativity-Intelligence Distinction_ (1965).

## **2. Main Concept: Creativity and Intelligence Are Distinct**

Wallach and Kogan argued that creativity and intelligence are related but distinguishable cognitive abilities.

Their central propositions were:

1. Creativity should not be treated as identical to intelligence.
2. Conventional intelligence tests cannot adequately measure creative potential.
3. Creativity involves generating numerous and unique associations.
4. Assessment conditions influence the expression of creative abilities.
5. Creative thinking should be assessed under conditions that encourage freedom of expression rather than fear of evaluation.

**Central idea:** Creativity is characterised by the ability to produce plentiful and unique associations in response to different stimuli.

## **3. Wallach and Kogan's Approach to Creativity**

Their approach was strongly influenced by **associative thinking**.

According to their perspective, creative individuals demonstrate the ability to produce numerous associations and unusual connections in response to a given stimulus.

Two major characteristics were emphasised:

### **A. Productivity (Fluency)**

- Refers to the total number of responses generated.
- A person who produces more relevant associations demonstrates greater productivity.

_Example:_ When asked to suggest uses for a brick, an individual generates 15 different uses.

### **B. Uniqueness (Originality)**

- Refers to the unusualness or rarity of responses.
- A person who produces uncommon associations demonstrates greater uniqueness.

_Example:_ Suggesting that a brick could be used as a component of a homemade musical instrument.

| Dimension    | Meaning                                |
| ------------ | -------------------------------------- |
| Productivity | Total number of responses              |
| Uniqueness   | Number or degree of uncommon responses |

**NET point:** Productivity and uniqueness are the two major scoring dimensions commonly associated with the Wallach–Kogan approach.

## **4. Wallach–Kogan Tests of Creativity**

Important assessment topic

Wallach and Kogan developed creativity tasks to assess children's ability to produce numerous and unique associations.

Their research used five major types of tasks.

| Test                | Description                                                    | Example                                        |
| ------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| 1. Instances        | Generate examples possessing a particular characteristic       | Name as many things as possible that are round |
| 2. Alternate Uses   | Suggest different uses for familiar objects                    | Give unusual uses for a brick                  |
| 3. Similarities     | Identify similarities between two apparently different objects | How are a potato and carrot similar?           |
| 4. Pattern Meanings | Suggest possible interpretations of abstract visual patterns   | What could this unusual shape represent?       |
| 5. Line Meanings    | Generate interpretations of different line drawings            | What could these lines represent?              |

### **Explanation of the five tasks**

**1. Instances**

The participant is asked to generate as many examples as possible of a particular category or characteristic.

Example: List things that are red, round or transparent.

**2. Alternate Uses**

The participant is asked to suggest different uses for ordinary objects.

Example: What are the different uses of a shoe besides wearing it?

**3. Similarities**

The participant identifies common characteristics between two different objects.

Example: In what ways are a train and tractor similar?

**4. Pattern Meanings**

The participant interprets abstract visual patterns and suggests possible meanings.

**5. Line Meanings**

The participant generates different interpretations of line drawings.

**Important:** These tasks assess associative productivity and uniqueness through verbal and visual stimuli.&#x20;

## **5. Importance of a Relaxed, Playful Environment**

This is the most distinctive feature of Wallach and Kogan's approach.

They argued that creativity assessment should occur in an environment that does not create excessive performance pressure.

### **Characteristics of the testing environment**

1. **Non-evaluative:** Participants should not feel that their responses are being judged as right or wrong.
2. **Untimed:** Avoid strict time limits that may restrict the natural flow of ideas.
3. **Playful:** Tasks should be presented as games rather than formal examinations.
4. **Relaxed:** Participants should feel comfortable expressing unusual ideas.
5. **Freedom of expression:** Individuals should be encouraged to produce as many responses as possible.

### **Why is this important?**

Conventional intelligence testing generally involves:

- Time limits.
- Formal instructions.
- Correct and incorrect answers.
- Performance evaluation.
- Competitive examination conditions.

Wallach and Kogan believed that these conditions could restrict creative expression.

They therefore recommended a more permissive atmosphere to encourage spontaneous and original responses.

**Wallach–Kogan's central assessment principle**

# **Playful + Untimed + Non-evaluative**

An environment designed to encourage the natural generation of creative associations.

## **6. Major Study and Findings (1965)**

### **Objective**

To examine whether creativity and intelligence represent separate dimensions of cognitive functioning.

### **Participants**

- The study involved **151 fifth-grade children**.
- Participants were approximately 10–11 years old.
- The children came from a public school district in New England.

### **Method**

- Creativity was assessed using verbal and visual associative tasks.
- Intelligence was assessed separately using conventional intelligence measures.
- Creativity tasks were administered individually in a relaxed, game-like atmosphere.
- Responses were evaluated in terms of productivity and uniqueness.

### **Major findings**

**A. Creativity and intelligence could be distinguished**

The researchers found that creativity measures were relatively independent of conventional intelligence measures in their sample.

**B. Creativity measures showed consistency**

Different creativity tasks demonstrated meaningful relationships with one another, suggesting that they assessed a related creative-thinking dimension.

**C. Testing conditions mattered**

The researchers argued that formal, evaluative and time-pressured testing could influence the expression of creative ability.

**D. Creative potential involves both quantity and uniqueness**

Generating numerous responses is important, but the unusualness of those responses is also significant.

## **7. Wallach and Kogan vs. Getzels and Jackson**

Both research groups examined the relationship between creativity and intelligence, but their approaches differed.

| Basis                 | Getzels & Jackson                                    | Wallach & Kogan                                                              |
| --------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Important year        | 1962                                                 | 1965                                                                         |
| Main focus            | Creativity and intelligence among gifted students    | Distinction between creativity and intelligence                              |
| Major work            | _Creativity and Intelligence_                        | _Modes of Thinking in Young Children_                                        |
| Creativity assessment | Five creativity tests                                | Five associative tasks                                                       |
| Testing environment   | More conventional testing conditions                 | Relaxed, playful and non-evaluative                                          |
| Major emphasis        | High creativity does not necessarily mean highest IQ | Assessment conditions influence the expression and measurement of creativity |
| Scoring emphasis      | Different creativity abilities                       | Productivity and uniqueness                                                  |

**NET trap:** Both researchers challenged the assumption that creativity is simply a function of high intelligence, but Wallach and Kogan particularly emphasised the importance of the testing environment.

## **9. Criticisms and Limitations**

Although Wallach and Kogan made an important contribution, their approach has certain limitations.

1. **Limited generalisability:** Their original study focused on a relatively restricted group of fifth-grade children.
2. **Assessment context:** The assumption that playful conditions always improve creativity has not received consistent support in subsequent research.
3. **Creativity measurement:** Productivity and uniqueness alone cannot represent every aspect of creativity.
4. **Predictive validity:** High scores on divergent-thinking tasks do not necessarily translate into significant creative achievements in real life.
5. **Intelligence relationship:** Later research suggests that creativity and intelligence are not completely unrelated; their association depends partly on the measures and samples used.

**Important qualification:** Wallach and Kogan's findings should not be interpreted as proving that creativity and intelligence are entirely independent.
