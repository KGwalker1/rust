# **Getzels and Jackson: Creativity and Intelligence**

A complete, exam-oriented note for your **UGC-NET JRF Psychology (December 2026)** preparation, covering their theory, research, creativity–intelligence relationship, major findings, comparison with Torrance and Guilford, NET pointers and MCQ traps.

## **1. Introduction and Background**

- **Jacob W. Getzels (1912–2001)** and **Philip W. Jackson (1928–2015)** were American educational psychologists.
- They are well known for their research examining the relationship between **creativity and intelligence**.
- Their major contribution was demonstrating that highly creative students do not necessarily have the highest intelligence-test scores.
- They challenged the assumption that intelligence and creativity are identical abilities.

**Important work:**

Getzels, J. W., & Jackson, P. W. (1962). _Creativity and Intelligence: Explorations with Gifted Students._

NET important

&#x20;The study was published in **1962** and examined intellectually gifted students.

## **2. Main Idea: Creativity and Intelligence**

Getzels and Jackson proposed that creativity and intelligence are related but distinct abilities.

Their research suggested that:

1. High intelligence does not automatically mean high creativity.
2. Highly creative individuals may not necessarily obtain the highest intelligence-test scores.
3. Intelligence tests and creativity tests measure different aspects of cognitive functioning.
4. Individuals with high creativity can perform successfully even when their IQ scores are lower than those of highly intelligent but less creative individuals.

**Central argument:** Creativity cannot be adequately understood or measured through intelligence tests alone.

## **3. The Getzels and Jackson Study (1962)**

### **Objective**

To investigate the relationship between intelligence and creativity among gifted schoolchildren.

### **Participants**

- The research involved students from a private school in Chicago.
- The participants were approximately 449 students from Grades 6–12.
- The researchers studied students who were considered intellectually gifted.

### **Method**

The researchers administered intelligence tests and creativity tests to assess different intellectual abilities.

They identified two broad groups:

1. High-IQ students.
2. High-creativity students.

The groups were compared in terms of their intellectual characteristics and academic performance.

### **Five Creativity Tests Used**

The study used five tests to assess creative thinking.

| Test                  | What it measured                                                       |
| --------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Word Association Test | Ability to produce associations with given words                       |
| Uses of Objects Test  | Ability to generate unusual uses for common objects                    |
| Hidden Shapes Test    | Ability to identify or perceive shapes embedded within complex figures |
| Fables Test           | Ability to create endings or interpretations for incomplete stories    |
| Make-up Problems Test | Ability to formulate problems based on given information               |

**NET point:** These tests assessed creative abilities beyond conventional intelligence-test performance.

## **4. Major Findings of Getzels and Jackson**

The study produced several important findings.

### **A. Creativity and Intelligence Are Different**

- High intelligence and high creativity were not necessarily found together.
- Some students who scored highly on creativity measures had lower IQ scores than students in the high-IQ group.
- Therefore, creativity could not be treated as merely another name for intelligence.

### **B. High-Creativity Students Could Perform Well Academically**

- High-creativity students demonstrated academic achievement comparable to high-IQ students in the context of the study.
- This challenged the assumption that superior academic performance always requires the highest IQ.

### **C. Different Cognitive Strengths**

The researchers highlighted differences in the thinking patterns of the two groups.

| High-IQ students                                         | High-creativity students                  |
| -------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| Strong performance on conventional intelligence measures | Strong performance on creativity measures |
| More associated with convergent thinking                 | More associated with divergent thinking   |
| Often focus on finding the correct answer                | Often generate multiple possible answers  |
| Conventional problem-solving strengths                   | Original and unconventional approaches    |

**Important qualification:** These are broad tendencies discussed in the research, not rigid personality categories. High-IQ individuals can also be highly creative.

## **5. High IQ vs. High Creativity: The Three-Group Classification**

A particularly important aspect of Getzels and Jackson's work is the distinction between students based on their IQ and creativity scores.

| Group                   | Characteristics                                                      |
| ----------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| High IQ                 | High intelligence-test scores, comparatively lower creativity scores |
| High Creativity         | High creativity scores, comparatively lower IQ scores                |
| High IQ–High Creativity | High scores on both intelligence and creativity measures             |

The study challenged the assumption that the first group necessarily represents the highest level of intellectual potential.

**NET point:** Creativity and intelligence should be assessed separately when identifying giftedness.

## **6. Divergent and Convergent Thinking**

Getzels and Jackson's research is frequently discussed alongside the distinction between divergent and convergent thinking.

### **Divergent Thinking**

- Producing multiple possible solutions.
- Generating unusual ideas.
- Exploring different approaches.
- Associated with creativity.

### **Convergent Thinking**

- Narrowing down possibilities.
- Identifying the most appropriate answer.
- Applying logical reasoning.
- Commonly assessed through conventional intelligence tests.

| Divergent Thinking        | Convergent Thinking            |
| ------------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| Multiple possible answers | One best or correct answer     |
| Open-ended problems       | Structured problems            |
| Fluency and originality   | Accuracy and logical reasoning |
| Creativity-oriented       | Intelligence-test-oriented     |

**Important:** Intelligence and creativity are not exclusively associated with convergent and divergent thinking, respectively. Both forms of thinking can contribute to creative problem-solving.

## **7. Educational Implications**

Getzels and Jackson's work has important implications for educational psychology.

### **A. Identification of Gifted Students**

- Schools should not identify giftedness solely through IQ scores.
- Creative abilities should also be assessed.
- Students with high creative potential may be overlooked by conventional intelligence testing.

### **B. Curriculum Development**

Educational programmes should provide opportunities for:

- Independent thinking.
- Problem formulation.
- Imagination and experimentation.
- Original problem-solving.
- Exploration of multiple answers.

### **C. Evaluation Methods**

- Traditional examinations often emphasise correct answers and memorisation.
- Creative abilities require open-ended assessment.
- Students should be encouraged to develop original approaches rather than merely reproduce information.

### **D. Role of Teachers**

Teachers should encourage curiosity, tolerate unusual questions and provide opportunities for creative expression.

## **8. Criticisms and Limitations**

Although Getzels and Jackson made an important contribution, their research had limitations.

1. **Limited sample:** Participants came from a particular private school, limiting generalisability.
2. **Measurement issues:** Creativity tests may not capture every dimension of creative ability.
3. **Creativity–intelligence relationship:** The study did not establish that intelligence and creativity are completely independent.
4. **Academic achievement:** Findings from a specific educational setting cannot automatically be generalised to all students.
5. **Creativity is multidimensional:** Creative achievement involves motivation, personality, knowledge, environmental opportunities and other factors, not merely test scores.

## **9. Comparison: Getzels and Jackson vs. Torrance vs. Guilford**

High-priority NET comparison

| Basis                    | Getzels & Jackson                                | E. Paul Torrance                               | J. P. Guilford                                               |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Major contribution       | Relationship between creativity and intelligence | Measurement and development of creativity      | Structure of Intellect model                                 |
| Important year           | 1962                                             | 1966 (TTCT)                                    | 1950 (APA address on creativity)                             |
| Main focus               | Creativity and giftedness                        | Creative thinking assessment                   | Divergent production                                         |
| Key concept              | Creativity is distinct from intelligence         | Fluency, flexibility, originality, elaboration | Divergent thinking                                           |
| Assessment               | Five creativity tests                            | Torrance Tests of Creative Thinking            | Structure of Intellect and divergent-thinking tasks          |
| Educational significance | Identification of creative giftedness            | Development of creative potential              | Recognition of divergent thinking as an intellectual ability |

### **Remember the distinctions**

- **Guilford:** Established the importance of divergent thinking in the study of creativity.
- **Getzels and Jackson:** Examined the relationship between creativity and intelligence.
- **Torrance:** Developed a widely used standardised assessment of creative thinking.
