# **Concept Formation: Rules, Types and Strategies**

## **1. Introduction**

- **Concept formation** is the cognitive process of organising objects, events and experiences into categories based on their common characteristics.
- It helps individuals classify information, recognise patterns and develop knowledge.
- A concept is a mental representation of a category or class of objects.
- Example: Grouping sparrows, pigeons and eagles under the concept of _birds_.

**Important psychologists:**

- **Jerome Bruner, Jacqueline Goodnow and George Austin (1956):** Concept attainment and strategies.
- **Eleanor Rosch:** Prototype theory of categorisation.
- **Jean Piaget:** Concept development through cognitive development.

## **2. Rules of Concept Formation**

According to Bruner, Goodnow and Austin (1956), concepts can be acquired by identifying the rules that determine category membership.

| Rule                 | Meaning                                                           | Example                                      |
| -------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| **Conjunctive rule** | All specified attributes must be present (AND).                   | A figure must be red AND circular.           |
| **Disjunctive rule** | At least one specified attribute must be present (OR).            | A figure must be red OR circular.            |
| **Relational rule**  | Category membership depends on a relationship between attributes. | A figure must be larger than another figure. |

**NET point:** Conjunctive = AND; Disjunctive = OR; Relational = relationship.

## **3. Types of Concepts**

| Type                     | Meaning                                                         | Example                         |
| ------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| **Concrete concepts**    | Based on observable objects.                                    | Chair, tree, dog.               |
| **Abstract concepts**    | Based on ideas and qualities.                                   | Freedom, justice, intelligence. |
| **Natural concepts**     | Formed through everyday experiences; boundaries may be unclear. | Birds, fruits, furniture.       |
| **Artificial concepts**  | Defined by specific rules or characteristics.                   | Triangle, square.               |
| **Conjunctive concepts** | Require two or more attributes together.                        | Red AND circular.               |
| **Disjunctive concepts** | Require one or more alternative attributes.                     | Red OR circular.                |
| **Relational concepts**  | Based on relationships between objects.                         | Bigger than, above, before.     |

## **4. Major Approaches to Concept Formation**

### **A. Classical Approach**

- Concepts are defined by **necessary and sufficient features**.
- Every member must possess the defining characteristics.
- Example: A triangle must have three sides.
- Limitation: Many natural categories have unclear boundaries.

### **B. Prototype Approach**

- Associated with **Eleanor Rosch (1970s)**.
- Concepts are represented by a typical or most representative example called a prototype.
- Objects are classified according to their similarity to the prototype.
- Example: A sparrow may be considered a more typical bird than a penguin.

### **C. Exemplar Approach**

- Concepts are formed through stored examples of category members.
- New objects are compared with previously encountered examples.
- Example: Recognising a new dog by comparing it with dogs seen earlier.

### **D. Theory-Based Approach**

- Concept formation depends on background knowledge and understanding of relationships.
- Individuals classify objects based on their understanding of how things work.
- Example: Understanding why whales are mammals despite living in water.

## **5. Strategies of Concept Formation**

Bruner, Goodnow and Austin (1956) studied how individuals identify concepts by testing hypotheses.

### **A. Scanning Strategies**

| Strategy                  | Meaning                                                   |
| ------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Simultaneous scanning** | Considering several possible hypotheses at the same time. |
| **Successive scanning**   | Testing hypotheses one after another.                     |

### **B. Focusing Strategies**

| Strategy                  | Meaning                                                                          |
| ------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Conservative focusing** | Changing one attribute at a time while keeping the other attributes constant.    |
| **Focus gambling**        | Changing or testing several attributes together to discover the concept quickly. |

**Important distinction:**

- Scanning involves testing possible hypotheses.
- Focusing involves using a positive example as a reference point.

### **C. Other Strategies**

- **Hypothesis testing:** Forming and testing possible rules.
- **Reception strategy:** Receiving examples and identifying the underlying rule.
- **Selection strategy:** Selecting examples to test a hypothesis.

## **6. Stages of Concept Formation**

1. **Observation:** Identifying objects and their characteristics.
2. **Comparison:** Finding similarities and differences.
3. **Abstraction:** Identifying common essential features.
4. **Generalisation:** Applying identified features to new examples.
5. **Classification:** Grouping objects into appropriate categories.
