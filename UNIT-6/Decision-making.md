# **Decision-Making: Types and Models**

## **1. Introduction**

- **Decision-making** is the cognitive process of selecting one option from two or more alternatives to achieve a particular goal.
- It involves identifying problems, evaluating alternatives, considering consequences and selecting the most appropriate course of action.
- It is an important component of cognition, problem-solving and executive functioning.

## **2. Types of Decision-Making**

| Type                            | Meaning                                                             | Example                                                    |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| **1. Programmed decisions**     | Routine decisions based on established rules or procedures.         | Following a daily study schedule.                          |
| **2. Non-programmed decisions** | New, complex and unfamiliar decisions.                              | Choosing a career.                                         |
| **3. Individual decisions**     | Decisions made by a single person.                                  | Selecting a subject.                                       |
| **4. Group decisions**          | Decisions made collectively by a group.                             | A committee selecting a candidate.                         |
| **5. Intuitive decisions**      | Decisions based on experience, feelings and immediate judgement.    | An experienced teacher recognising a student's difficulty. |
| **6. Rational decisions**       | Decisions based on logical analysis and evaluation of alternatives. | Comparing different universities before admission.         |
| **7. Personal decisions**       | Decisions affecting an individual's personal life.                  | Choosing a lifestyle.                                      |
| **8. Organisational decisions** | Decisions related to organisational functioning.                    | Recruitment and resource allocation.                       |

## **3. Major Models of Decision-Making**

### **A. Rational Decision-Making Model**

- Associated with classical economic and rational-choice approaches.
- Assumes that individuals make decisions through systematic evaluation.
- The decision-maker attempts to select the option that maximises benefits.

**Steps:**

1. Identify the problem.
2. Establish objectives.
3. Identify alternatives.
4. Evaluate alternatives.
5. Select the best alternative.
6. Implement the decision.
7. Evaluate the outcome.

**Limitation:** Assumes sufficient information, time and cognitive capacity, which are often unavailable in real situations.

### **B. Bounded Rationality Model**

- Proposed by **Herbert A. Simon (1955)**.
- Individuals have limited information, time and cognitive abilities.
- Therefore, they cannot always identify the objectively best option.
- They use **satisficing**: selecting an option that is good enough to meet minimum requirements.

**Important term:** Satisficing = Satisfying + Sufficing.

### **C. Incremental Model**

- Associated with **Charles E. Lindblom (1959)**.
- Decisions are made through small, gradual changes rather than complete analysis.
- Existing policies or decisions are modified incrementally.
- Also called the **muddling through approach**.

Example: Improving an existing educational policy through small adjustments.

### **D. Intuitive Decision-Making Model**

- Decisions are based on experience, recognition and immediate judgement.
- Requires less deliberate analytical processing.
- Particularly useful when quick decisions are needed.
- Intuition is not necessarily irrational; it can reflect accumulated experience.

### **E. Garbage Can Model**

- Developed by **Michael D. Cohen, James G. March and Johan P. Olsen (1972)**.
- Explains decision-making in organisations with unclear goals and uncertain procedures.
- Decisions emerge from the interaction of four independent streams:

1. **Problems:** Issues requiring solutions.
2. **Solutions:** Ideas or proposed answers.
3. **Participants:** People involved in decision-making.
4. **Choice opportunities:** Situations in which decisions can be made.

**Key idea:** Solutions may exist before problems arise, and decisions can emerge through chance interactions among the four streams.

### **F. Prospect Theory**

- Developed by **Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky (1979)**.
- Explains how people make decisions under risk and uncertainty.
- People evaluate outcomes relative to a reference point rather than only considering final wealth.
- Losses generally have a greater psychological impact than equivalent gains.

**Important concepts:**

- **Loss aversion:** Losses feel more significant than equivalent gains.
- **Framing effect:** Decisions change depending on how information is presented.
- **Diminishing sensitivity:** The psychological impact of changes decreases as values move further from the reference point.

## **4. Other Important Decision-Making Concepts**

| Concept                          | Meaning                                                                                       |
| -------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Heuristics**                   | Mental shortcuts used to make quick decisions.                                                |
| **Availability heuristic**       | Judging probability based on how easily examples come to mind.                                |
| **Representativeness heuristic** | Judging likelihood based on similarity to a typical example.                                  |
| **Anchoring bias**               | Relying heavily on the first information received.                                            |
| **Confirmation bias**            | Seeking information that supports existing beliefs.                                           |
| **Overconfidence bias**          | Overestimating one's knowledge or decision-making ability.                                    |
| **Sunk cost fallacy**            | Continuing an action because of previously invested resources, despite poor future prospects. |

## **5. Dual-Process Theory of Decision-Making**

Associated particularly with **Daniel Kahneman**.

| System 1                     | System 2                     |
| ---------------------------- | ---------------------------- |
| Fast thinking                | Slow thinking                |
| Automatic                    | Deliberate                   |
| Intuitive                    | Analytical                   |
| Requires less effort         | Requires more effort         |
| Often operates unconsciously | Involves conscious reasoning |

- System 1 produces quick judgements and intuitive responses.
- System 2 involves deliberate reasoning, calculation and evaluation.
- Both systems interact during decision-making.

**Reference:** Kahneman's _Thinking, Fast and Slow_ (2011).
