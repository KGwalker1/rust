# **Problem-Solving: Types, Strategies and Obstacles**

## **1. Introduction**

- **Problem-solving** is a cognitive process through which an individual identifies a problem and develops strategies to reach a desired goal.
- It involves reasoning, memory, creativity, decision-making and critical thinking.
- According to **Robert Gagné (1970)**, problem-solving is a higher-order intellectual skill involving the application of previously learned rules to solve new problems.

## **2. Types of Problem-Solving**

| Type                         | Meaning                                                            | Example                                                |
| ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| **1. Well-defined problems** | Problems with clearly specified goals, rules and solutions.        | Solving a mathematical equation.                       |
| **2. Ill-defined problems**  | Problems with unclear goals, rules or possible solutions.          | Deciding how to reduce unemployment.                   |
| **3. Routine problems**      | Problems solved using previously learned methods.                  | Solving a familiar arithmetic problem.                 |
| **4. Non-routine problems**  | Problems requiring new approaches and strategies.                  | Developing a new scientific solution.                  |
| **5. Insight problems**      | Problems solved through sudden understanding or restructuring.     | Solving a puzzle after suddenly recognising a pattern. |
| **6. Analytical problems**   | Problems solved through systematic reasoning and logical analysis. | Analysing a research problem.                          |
| **7. Creative problems**     | Problems requiring original and innovative solutions.              | Designing a new product.                               |

## **3. Major Strategies of Problem-Solving**

### **A. Trial and Error**

- Associated with **Edward L. Thorndike**.
- Involves trying different responses until a successful solution is found.
- Learning occurs through repeated attempts and consequences.
- Example: Trying different keys to open a lock.

### **B. Algorithm**

- A systematic sequence of steps that guarantees a correct solution when correctly applied to a suitable problem.
- Usually requires more time than heuristics.
- Example: Following mathematical procedures to solve an equation.

### **C. Heuristics**

- Mental shortcuts or general strategies used to solve problems quickly.
- They do not guarantee a correct solution.
- Useful when complete information or time is unavailable.

Important heuristics:

| Heuristic              | Meaning                                                                |
| ---------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Means-end analysis** | Reducing the difference between the current state and the goal state.  |
| **Working backward**   | Starting from the desired goal and moving towards the initial state.   |
| **Hill climbing**      | Choosing steps that appear to bring the individual closer to the goal. |
| **Analogy**            | Using the solution to a previous similar problem.                      |
| **Subgoaling**         | Dividing a large problem into smaller manageable problems.             |

### **D. Insight Learning**

- Associated with **Wolfgang Köhler (1925)**.
- Involves sudden understanding of the relationship between different elements of a problem.
- Often described as an **Aha! experience**.
- Köhler demonstrated insight learning through experiments with chimpanzees.

### **E. Analogy-Based Problem-Solving**

- Involves applying knowledge from a familiar problem to a new but structurally similar problem.
- Example: Using knowledge of one mathematical problem to solve a similar problem with different numbers.

### **F. Brainstorming**

- A creative problem-solving technique involving the generation of many possible ideas.
- Associated with **Alex F. Osborn (1953)**.
- Encourages free expression of ideas without immediate criticism.

## **4. Stages of Problem-Solving**

A commonly used framework is **Wallas's Four Stages of Creative Problem-Solving (1926)**.

| Stage               | Meaning                                                                                             |
| ------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **1. Preparation**  | Identifying the problem and collecting relevant information.                                        |
| **2. Incubation**   | Temporarily stepping away from the problem while unconscious or background processing may continue. |
| **3. Illumination** | Sudden emergence of a possible solution or insight.                                                 |
| **4. Verification** | Testing and evaluating the proposed solution.                                                       |

**Remember:** Preparation → Incubation → Illumination → Verification.

## **5. Obstacles to Problem-Solving**

| Obstacle                      | Meaning                                                           | Example                                                     |
| ----------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| **1. Mental set**             | Using a previously successful method even when it is unsuitable.  | Repeatedly applying the same method to a new problem.       |
| **2. Functional fixedness**   | Seeing an object only in terms of its usual function.             | Not considering a box as a possible step or support.        |
| **3. Confirmation bias**      | Focusing on information that supports existing beliefs.           | Ignoring evidence that contradicts an initial solution.     |
| **4. Irrelevant information** | Being distracted by information unrelated to the problem.         | Focusing on unnecessary details in a mathematical question. |
| **5. Lack of motivation**     | Insufficient willingness to continue solving a difficult problem. | Giving up after repeated failures.                          |
| **6. Emotional factors**      | Anxiety, fear and frustration interfering with reasoning.         | Examination anxiety affecting problem-solving.              |
| **7. Incomplete knowledge**   | Lack of necessary information or skills.                          | Being unable to solve a problem due to missing concepts.    |

## **6. Important Psychological Experiments**

### **A. Duncker's Candle Problem (1945)**

- Conducted by **Karl Duncker**.
- Participants were asked to attach a candle to a wall without allowing wax to drip.
- Demonstrated **functional fixedness**.
- The solution involved using a box of tacks as a candle holder.

### **B. Two-String Problem (1945)**

- Also associated with **Norman Maier**.
- Participants had to connect two strings that were too far apart to hold simultaneously.
- The solution involved using an object as a weight to swing one string.
- Demonstrated how mental set and functional fixedness can interfere with problem-solving.

### **C. Water-Jug Problem**

- Associated with **Abraham Luchins (1942)**.
- Participants solved water-jar problems using repeated procedures.
- Demonstrated **Einstellung effect**, in which a familiar solution strategy prevents discovery of a simpler alternative.

## **9. Common MCQ Traps**

- **Mental set:** Dependence on a previously successful strategy.
- **Functional fixedness:** Inability to see alternative uses of an object.
- **Einstellung effect:** An established solution prevents discovery of a better one.
- **Insight:** Sudden understanding of a solution.
- **Algorithm:** Systematic procedure that guarantees a solution when correctly applied.
- **Heuristic:** Shortcut that may not guarantee a solution.
- **Incubation:** Temporary withdrawal from conscious work on a problem.
- **Illumination:** Sudden emergence of a solution.

## **10. Last-Minute Revision**

Remember the three major areas:

**Types:** Well-defined, ill-defined, routine, non-routine and insight problems.

**Strategies:** Trial and error, algorithms, heuristics, insight, analogy and brainstorming.

**Obstacles:** Mental set, functional fixedness, confirmation bias, emotional interference and lack of knowledge.

**One-line summary:** Problem-solving is a goal-directed cognitive process involving different strategies, such as algorithms, heuristics and insight, which may be hindered by mental set, functional fixedness and other cognitive obstacles.
