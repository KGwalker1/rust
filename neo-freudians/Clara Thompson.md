## 2. Cultural psychoanalysis

Thompson belonged to the cultural/interpersonal tradition, which emphasized how culture and relationships shape personality.

Its central ideas:

- Culture shapes personality: psychological difficulties cannot be explained solely by biological drives or instinctual conflicts.
- The Oedipus complex is culturally relative: it is not necessarily universal in the same form across different family structures and cultures.
- Psychoanalytic theories reflect their cultural context: Freud’s theories were influenced by Victorian Vienna and its social norms.
- Personality develops throughout life: social relationships continue to influence development beyond early childhood.

## 3. Feminine psychology — Thompson’s distinctive contribution

Thompson challenged Freud’s account of female development, arguing that it treated masculinity as the norm and defined women in terms of what they supposedly lacked.

| Freud’s position as presented in the source                         | Thompson’s critique                                   |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| Femininity explained substantially through anatomy and penis envy   | Female psychology must be understood on its own terms |
| Masculinity treated as the standard                                 | This reflects theoretical bias                        |
| Traits such as passivity and masochism linked to female development | These traits may be shaped by cultural expectations   |
| Penis envy interpreted anatomically                                 | Envy may concern male status and privilege            |

Thompson drew on cross-cultural anthropology, including the work of Margaret Mead and Ruth Benedict, to argue that femininity and masculinity are culturally shaped rather than fixed biological destinies.&#x20;

## 4. The analyst’s personality in treatment (1938)

This is Thompson’s most important specific contribution for examination questions.

Her paper argued that psychoanalytic treatment is not a purely technical or neutral procedure. The analyst’s personality, emotional responses, and relationship with the patient influence the therapeutic process.

| Concept               | Thompson’s position                                                                     |
| --------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Analyst’s personality | A therapeutic factor in its own right                                                   |
| Countertransference   | Can provide useful information about the patient                                        |
| Therapeutic technique | Should allow flexibility rather than rigid rule-following                               |
| Analytic relationship | A two-person process; healing occurs through the relationship as well as interpretation |

## 5. Thompson’s approach to therapy

The goal is to understand the patient’s interpersonal patterns within their cultural and family context.

- Examine the here-and-now relationship between therapist and patient.
- Treat the analyst as a real, responding person rather than a blank screen.
- Use flexible techniques, warmth, directness, and empathy.
- Explore cultural and gender-role expectations that contribute to psychological difficulties.

## 7. Contributions and criticisms

Contributions

- Advanced cultural and interpersonal approaches to psychoanalysis.
- Challenged male-centred explanations of female psychology.
- Highlighted the therapeutic significance of the analyst’s personality and countertransference.
- Helped establish the William Alanson White Institute as a centre for interpersonal psychoanalysis.

Criticisms

- Did not develop a comprehensive, independent personality theory.
- Some cross-cultural claims were broad and based on limited anthropological evidence.
- Many of her arguments were clinically and philosophically influential but lacked rigorous experimental testing.
