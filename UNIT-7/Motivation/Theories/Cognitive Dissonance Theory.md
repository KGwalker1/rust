### 🎯 Cognitive Dissonance Theory (Festinger, 1957)

**1. Core Idea**

- **Definition**: Leon Festinger proposed that when a person holds two or more cognitions (beliefs, attitudes, behaviors) that are **psychologically inconsistent**, it creates an aversive state of tension called **cognitive dissonance**. This discomfort motivates the person to reduce the inconsistency .
- **Key Insight**: The theory was famously inspired by Festinger's observation of a doomsday cult. When the prophesied flood failed to occur, committed members who were together did not abandon their belief; instead, they **proselytized harder** to gain social support for their cognition .

**2. How Dissonance is Reduced (Must Know for NET)**
People are motivated to eliminate dissonance through three main routes :

1.  **Change the Behavior**: Stop the inconsistent action (e.g., quit smoking if you believe it's harmful).
2.  **Change the Cognition**: Rationalize or deny (e.g., "smoking filtered cigarettes reduces the risk").
3.  **Add New Cognitions**: Justify the behavior with new beliefs (e.g., "smoking helps me stay thin, which is healthy").

**3. Key Research Paradigms (Classic Studies)**

| Paradigm                   | Core Finding                                                                                                                                                                                                 | Classic Study                                                                                                                                                      |
| :------------------------- | :----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Induced Compliance**     | Being **underpaid** to say something you don't believe causes more attitude change than being well-paid. A small reward is insufficient justification, so you change your attitude to justify your behavior. | Festinger & Carlsmith (1959): Participants paid $1 vs. $20 to tell someone a boring task was interesting. The $1 group actually rated the task as more enjoyable . |
| **Free-Choice**            | After making a **difficult decision** between two close alternatives, people **"spread the alternatives"**—rating the chosen option as more desirable and the rejected one as less desirable.                | Brehm (1956): Women choosing between household products rated their chosen item higher and rejected item lower after deciding .                                    |
| **Effort Justification**   | The more unpleasant the effort required to achieve a goal, the more people **value the outcome** to justify the suffering.                                                                                   | Aronson & Mills (1959): Women who underwent a **severe initiation** to join a boring group rated the group more favorably than those with a mild initiation .      |
| **Belief Disconfirmation** | When a strongly held belief is disconfirmed, people may **proselytize** to gain social support for the belief rather than abandon it.                                                                        | Festinger et al. (1956): The "When Prophecy Fails" doomsday cult study .                                                                                           |

**4. Key Theoretical Debates & Revisions**

- **Self-Perception Theory (Bem)**: Argued that dissonance effects are not driven by aversive arousal but by people **inferring their attitudes from their behavior**, like an outside observer. Later research found physiological arousal (skin conductance) does occur, supporting Festinger's original view .
- **Self-Consistency Revision (Aronson)**: Argued that dissonance is not just about any inconsistency, but specifically about inconsistency between behavior and the **self-concept** (e.g., "I am a good, rational person") .
- **Self-Affirmation (Steele)**: Suggested people can reduce dissonance by affirming unrelated positive aspects of the self, not necessarily by changing the specific attitude .

**5. Criticisms (Quick Points)**

- **Methodological Flaws**: Early studies were criticized for confounding variables and faulty statistical analysis .
- **Replication Issues**: A recent large-scale replication of the induced-compliance paradigm found weaker effects than originally reported, raising questions about the robustness of some classic findings .
- **Cultural Generalizability**: Most research is Western; the experience and reduction of dissonance may differ in collectivist cultures.
