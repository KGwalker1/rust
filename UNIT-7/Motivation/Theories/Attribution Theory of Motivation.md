### 🎯 Attribution Theory of Motivation (Weiner)

**1. Core Idea**

- **Definition**: Bernard Weiner’s attributional theory of motivation proposes that individuals search for the **causes** of their successes and failures, and these causal explanations—not the outcomes themselves—determine subsequent emotions, expectations, and motivated behavior .
- **Key Insight**: The theory is rooted in Fritz Heider’s (1958) foundational work on perceived causality, but Weiner extended it specifically to **achievement motivation**, making it one of the most cited and empirically supported frameworks in educational and motivational psychology .

**2. The Three Causal Dimensions (Must Know for NET)**

| Dimension                                             | Meaning                                                                                          | Key Consequence                                                                                                                                   |
| :---------------------------------------------------- | :----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Locus** (Internal vs. External)                     | Whether the cause resides within the person (ability, effort) or outside (task difficulty, luck) | **Pride/self-esteem**: Internal attributions for success increase pride; external attributions reduce it                                          |
| **Stability** (Stable vs. Unstable)                   | Whether the cause is consistent over time (aptitude) or variable (effort, mood)                  | **Expectancy change**: Stable attributions for failure lead to expectations of continued failure; unstable attributions suggest things can change |
| **Controllability** (Controllable vs. Uncontrollable) | Whether the cause can be volitionally altered (effort is controllable; aptitude is not)          | **Interpersonal judgments**: Controllable causes (e.g., lack of effort) elicit anger/blame; uncontrollable causes elicit pity/help                |

**3. The Classic Attribution Sequence**

- **Outcome** (success or failure) → **Causal search** (especially after unexpected or negative events) → **Attribution** (e.g., “I failed because I lack ability”) → **Dimensional appraisal** (internal, stable, uncontrollable) → **Psychological consequences** (shame, lowered expectancy, helplessness) → **Motivated behavior** (withdrawal, reduced effort) .
- **Key Example**: A student who attributes failure to **low ability** (internal, stable, uncontrollable) is likely to feel shame, expect future failure, and give up. A student who attributes the same failure to **lack of effort** (internal, unstable, controllable) may feel guilt but remains motivated to try harder .

**4. Key Concepts to Remember**

- **Intrapersonal vs. Interpersonal Theory**: Weiner distinguished between attributions people make about **their own outcomes** (affecting self-directed emotions like pride and guilt) and attributions **observers make about others** (affecting judgments like blame, help-giving, and punishment) .
- **Learned Helplessness Link**: An attributional style of explaining negative events as **internal, stable, and global** is associated with depression and helplessness .
- **Attributional Antecedents**: Causal search is triggered by **unexpected** or **negative** outcomes; people are more likely to ask “why?” when they fail than when they succeed .

**5. Criticisms (Quick Points)**

- **Consciousness of Process**: Critics question whether people are always consciously aware of the dimensional properties of their attributions .
- **Emotion Generation**: The theory is better at predicting specific achievement emotions (pride, shame, guilt) than explaining how “cold” cognitions transform into “hot” emotional experiences .
- **Cultural Variation**: The meaning and value of specific attributions (e.g., effort vs. ability) may differ across cultural contexts.

---

**Exam Tip**: Remember **Weiner = Attribution Theory**. Link the **three dimensions (Locus, Stability, Controllability)** to their specific consequences: **Locus → Pride/Shame**, **Stability → Expectancy**, **Controllability → Blame/Help**. The classic contrast is **Ability** (internal, stable, uncontrollable) vs. **Effort** (internal, unstable, controllable)—knowing which attribution a person makes predicts their emotional and behavioral response.
