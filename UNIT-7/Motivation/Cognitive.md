This approach emerged as a reaction against the mechanistic S-R and drive-reduction models, arguing that motivation is guided by **expectations, beliefs, attributions, and conscious goals** rather than by blind reinforcement or unconscious instincts.

## Cognitive Approach to Motivation (Detailed Preparation Note)

### 1. Core Idea and Historical Context

The cognitive approach views humans as **active information processors** who are motivated by their **interpretations, expectations, and evaluations** of situations. Unlike the S-R approach (which sees behavior as controlled by past reinforcement) or the psychoanalytical approach (which sees behavior as driven by unconscious instincts), the cognitive approach argues that motivation is **prospective**—it is directed toward future goals based on what the person believes will happen.

The core premise, as summarized in educational psychology literature: **motivation to reach a goal is the product of our expectations for success and the value of the goal to us. If either is zero, our motivation is zero** .

Key theorists associated with this approach include **Atkinson (expectancy-value)**, **Weiner (attribution)**, **Bandura (self-efficacy)**, and **Locke & Latham (goal-setting)**.

---

### 2. Expectancy-Value Theory (Atkinson)

**Core Idea**: Achievement motivation is a function of two factors: the **perceived probability of success** and the **subjective value** or incentive value of the task .

**Atkinson's (1957) Model**:

- Motivation to achieve = **Motive × Expectancy × Incentive**
- **Motive**: A stable disposition to strive for success (or to avoid failure).
- **Expectancy**: The perceived probability of success.
- **Incentive**: The value of the reward associated with success.

**Key Implication for NET**: If either expectancy or value is zero, motivation is zero. A student who believes they cannot succeed (expectancy = 0) or who does not care about the outcome (value = 0) will not be motivated .

**Later Development (Eccles et al.)**: Expanded the model to include **subjective task values** (intrinsic value, utility value, attainment value, and cost). A critical addition for NET is the concept of **cost**—the perceived negative consequences of engaging in a task (e.g., time, effort, anxiety) can independently reduce motivation even when expectancy and value are high .

---

### 3. Attribution Theory (Weiner)

**Core Idea**: Motivation is influenced by the **causes to which people attribute their successes and failures**. These attributions shape expectations for future success and emotional reactions, which in turn affect persistence and effort .

**The Three Causal Dimensions (High-Yield for NET)**:

| Dimension           | Meaning                                    | Psychological Consequence                             |
| :------------------ | :----------------------------------------- | :---------------------------------------------------- |
| **Locus**           | Is the cause internal or external?         | Affects **esteem-related emotions** (pride vs. shame) |
| **Stability**       | Is the cause stable or unstable over time? | Affects **expectancy of future success**              |
| **Controllability** | Is the cause under one's control?          | Affects **interpersonal judgments** (anger, sympathy) |

**Key Application**: A student who attributes failure to **lack of effort** (internal, unstable, controllable) is more likely to persist than one who attributes it to **lack of ability** (internal, stable, uncontrollable). The latter attribution leads to **learned helplessness** and reduced motivation.

**For NET**: Weiner's theory is frequently tested through scenarios asking which attributional dimension predicts persistence or emotional reactions.

---

### 4. Self-Efficacy Theory (Bandura)

**Core Idea**: **Self-efficacy** is the belief in one's capabilities to organize and execute the actions required to achieve a goal. It is a key motivational construct that affects **choice of activities, effort, persistence, and achievement** .

**Position in Social Cognitive Theory**: Self-efficacy operates within **triadic reciprocal determinism**—personal factors (cognitions), behavior, and environmental factors all influence each other .

**Four Sources of Self-Efficacy (High-Yield)**:

1. **Mastery experiences**: Previous successes—the most powerful source.
2. **Vicarious experiences**: Observing similar others succeed.
3. **Verbal persuasion**: Encouragement from others.
4. **Physiological and emotional states**: Anxiety or calmness influence efficacy beliefs .

**For NET**: Distinguish self-efficacy (belief about **capability** for a specific task) from self-esteem (belief about **self-worth** as a person) and from Rotter's locus of control (generalized belief about **who controls outcomes**) .

---

### 5. Goal-Setting Theory (Locke & Latham)

**Core Idea**: **Specific, challenging goals** lead to higher performance than vague or easy goals. Goals motivate by directing attention, mobilizing effort, encouraging persistence, and promoting strategy development .

**Key Principles for NET**:

- **Specificity**: Specific goals (e.g., "score 80%") outperform vague goals ("do your best").
- **Difficulty**: Challenging but attainable goals produce the highest performance.
- **Feedback**: Goals are most effective when accompanied by feedback on progress.
- **Commitment**: Goal commitment is necessary for goals to be effective.

**For NET**: Goal-setting theory is one of the most empirically supported theories in organizational psychology, developed by Locke (1968) and refined with Latham over decades .

### 🔑 High-Yield NET Traps and Comparisons

**1. Cognitive vs. S-R Approach**

- **S-R**: Motivation is controlled by **past reinforcement** (retrospective, mechanistic).
- **Cognitive**: Motivation is controlled by **expectations and future goals** (prospective, rational) .

**2. Atkinson vs. Weiner**

- **Atkinson**: Expectancy × Value (focus on **probability of success** and **incentive value**).
- **Weiner**: Attributions for success/failure (focus on **causal dimensions** and their emotional/expectancy consequences).

**3. Self-Efficacy vs. Locus of Control**

- **Self-efficacy (Bandura)**: "Can I do this specific task?" (domain-specific capability belief).
- **Locus of control (Rotter)**: "Who controls outcomes in general—me or external forces?" (generalized attributional belief).

**4. Goal-Setting Theory's Central Claim**
The most testable prediction: **Specific, difficult goals lead to higher performance than easy or vague goals**—provided the person is committed and receives feedback.
