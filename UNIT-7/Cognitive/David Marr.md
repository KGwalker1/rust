# **David Marr's Computational Theory of Vision — Quick Revision Notes**

## **1. Introduction & Background**

- **David Courtnay Marr (1945–1980):** British neuroscientist and psychologist, known for his contributions to computational neuroscience and visual perception.
- Worked at the **Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT)**.
- His major work, _**Vision: A Computational Investigation into the Human Representation and Processing of Visual Information**_**&#x20;(1982)**, was published posthumously.
- Marr viewed vision as an information-processing task involving the transformation of visual input into meaningful representations of the environment.

**NET association:** David Marr = Computational theory of vision + Three levels of analysis + Primal sketch → 2½-D sketch → 3-D model.

## **2. Core Philosophy — Vision as Information Processing**

Marr argued that understanding a complex information-processing system requires examining it at three distinct levels.

### **The Three Levels of Analysis**

Level 1

**Computational Theory — What and Why?**

Identifies the problem to be solved, the goal of computation and why the particular computational strategy is appropriate.

Level 2

**Representation and Algorithm — How?**

Explains how information is represented and which algorithm or procedures are used to perform the computation.

Level 3

**Hardware Implementation — Physical Realisation**

Explains how the representations and algorithms are physically implemented, such as through neural structures or electronic systems.

**NET memory trick:**

- Computational = What and Why?
- Algorithmic = How?
- Implementation = Where and physically how?

**Important:** Marr argued that these levels could be investigated relatively independently. Understanding a computational problem does not necessarily require knowing its precise neural implementation.

## **3. Three Representational Stages of Vision**

Marr proposed that visual processing transforms the initial retinal image into increasingly sophisticated representations.

**RAW VISUAL IMAGE**

Retinal intensity information

**1. PRIMAL SKETCH**

Edges, lines, boundaries and intensity changes

**2. 2½-D SKETCH**

Viewer-centred surfaces, depth and orientation

**3. 3-D MODEL**

Object-centred, three-dimensional representation

### **Stage 1: Primal Sketch**

- **Input:** Retinal image represented as a grey-level intensity array.
- Extracts basic visual features such as:
  - Edges
  - Lines
  - Boundaries
  - Blobs and intensity changes
- Makes important structural features of the image explicit.

**Remember:** Primal sketch = Basic visual features.

### **Stage 2: 2½-D Sketch**

- Provides a **viewer-centred representation** of visible surfaces.
- Contains information about:
  - Depth
  - Surface orientation
  - Distance relative to the observer
  - Visible surface boundaries
- Uses visual information such as binocular disparity and motion.

**Why 2½-D?** It does not provide a complete representation of the entire object. It describes visible surfaces relative to the observer's viewpoint.

**Remember:** 2½-D sketch = Viewer-centred representation.

### **Stage 3: 3-D Model**

- Produces an **object-centred, three-dimensional representation**.
- Represents objects independently of the observer's particular viewpoint.
- Describes their shape and spatial structure.
- Marr proposed **generalized cones** as important shape primitives for representing objects.

**Generalized cones:** Three-dimensional shapes generated by moving a cross-section along an axis, allowing complex shapes to be represented using simpler components.

**Remember:** 3-D model = Object-centred representation.

## **4. Marr vs. Gibson — Computational and Ecological Approaches**

| Dimension          | David Marr                        | James J. Gibson                           |
| ------------------ | --------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| Approach           | Computational                     | Ecological                                |
| Perception         | Information processing            | Direct pickup                             |
| Main emphasis      | Representations and algorithms    | Environmental information                 |
| Visual information | Requires computational processing | Rich and sufficient for direct perception |
| Key contribution   | Three levels of analysis          | Affordances                               |
| Key terminology    | Primal sketch, 2½-D, 3-D          | Optic array, invariants, affordances      |

**Important distinction:** Marr's computational approach and Gibson's ecological approach are theoretically different, but they should not automatically be treated as completely incompatible.

## **5. Contributions**

1. **Computational vision:** Established an influential framework for understanding visual perception as information processing.
2. **Three levels of analysis:** Provided a framework for studying complex cognitive systems.
3. **Visual representation:** Proposed the progression from primal sketch to 2½-D sketch to 3-D model.
4. **Computational neuroscience:** Made foundational contributions to understanding information processing in neural systems.
5. **Artificial intelligence:** Influenced computer vision, robotics and machine perception.

## **6. Criticisms**

1. **Incomplete 3-D model:** The transition from the 2½-D sketch to a complete object-centred representation remains theoretically and computationally challenging.
2. **Generalized cones:** These are not sufficient to represent every possible object shape.
3. **Bottom-up emphasis:** Marr's framework gives substantial attention to visual input and computational processing, while later research also emphasised top-down influences and contextual information.
4. **Independence of levels:** Critics question whether computational, algorithmic and implementation levels can always be studied independently.

## **8. Likely NET MCQ Traps**

- **Marr's three levels:** Computational, representation/algorithm and implementation.
- **Computational vs. algorithmic:** Computational asks what and why; algorithmic asks how.
- **Primal sketch:** First representation, containing basic visual features.
- **2½-D sketch:** Viewer-centred, not object-centred.
- **3-D model:** Object-centred and viewpoint-independent.
- **Generalized cones:** Associated with Marr's representation of three-dimensional shape.
- **Publication year:** _Vision_ was published in 1982, two years after Marr's death.
- **Gibson vs. Marr:** Gibson = direct ecological perception; Marr = computational analysis of vision.
- **Feature Integration Theory:** Associated with Anne Treisman, not Marr.
- **Affordances:** Associated with Gibson, not Marr.
