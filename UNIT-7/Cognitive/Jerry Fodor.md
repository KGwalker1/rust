# **Jerry Fodor – Cognitive Architecture (Language of Thought & Modularity of Mind)**

_UGC-NET JRF Psychology — Short Revision Notes_

## **1. Introduction**

- **Jerry Fodor (1935–2017):** American philosopher and cognitive scientist; major figure in classical computationalism.
- Key works:
  - _The Language of Thought_ (1975) – Language of Thought Hypothesis (LOTH).
  - _The Modularity of Mind_ (1983) – modularity of input systems.
  - _Concepts: Where Cognitive Science Went Wrong_ (1998) – conceptual atomism.
- **Major debate:** Fodor and Pylyshyn (1988) challenged connectionism over the systematicity of cognition.

## **2. Classical Computationalism**

Fodor argued that **cognition involves computation over structured mental representations**.

- **Representational Theory of Mind (RTM):** Beliefs and desires are relations to mental representations.
- **Classicism:** The mind operates like a symbol-processing computer.
- **Syntax:** Formal structure of mental representations.
- **Semantics:** Meaning of representations.
- Computation operates on syntax while representations carry meaning.

**NET link:** Similar to Newell and Simon's Physical Symbol System Hypothesis (PSSH).

## **3. Language of Thought Hypothesis (LOTH) – 1975**

**Core idea:** Thinking occurs in an innate, language-like mental code called **mentalese**, distinct from natural languages.

Key features:

1. **Compositionality:** Complex thoughts are constructed from simpler mental representations.
2. **Innateness:** Mentalese is innate and universal; it is the medium through which learning and thinking occur.
3. **Causation by content:** Mental states cause behaviour because of their representational content.

### **Systematicity Argument – Fodor & Pylyshyn (1988)**

- **Systematicity:** The ability to think one thought implies the ability to think structurally related thoughts.
- Example: Someone who can think "John loves Mary" can also think "Mary loves John."
- Fodor and Pylyshyn argued that classical, compositional representations explain systematicity.
- They challenged connectionism for failing to adequately explain this feature of cognition.

**NET keywords:** Mentalese, compositionality, systematicity, Fodor and Pylyshyn (1988).

## **4. Modularity of Mind – 1983**

Fodor distinguished between two types of cognitive systems:

| Input systems                      | Central systems                               |
| ---------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| Modular                            | Non-modular                                   |
| Perception and language processing | Thought and reasoning                         |
| Domain-specific                    | Relatively holistic                           |
| Automatic processing               | Integrates information from different sources |

### **Properties of Fodorian Modules**

1. **Domain-specific:** Process particular kinds of information.
2. **Mandatory:** Operate automatically when triggered.
3. **Informationally encapsulated:** Cannot freely access higher-level beliefs and knowledge.
4. **Limited central access:** Have proprietary databases.
5. **Fast processing:** Operate rapidly.
6. **Fixed neural architecture:** Have dedicated neural substrates.

**Important distinction:** Fodor proposed modularity mainly for peripheral input systems, not central reasoning.

**NET trap:** Informational encapsulation is a defining feature of Fodorian modules.

## **5. Conceptual Atomism – 1998**

**Core idea:** Most concepts are atomic, meaning they lack internal semantic structure.

- Conceptual content is determined by **informational relations between the mind and the world**.
- Concepts are not necessarily reducible to definitions, prototypes or theories.
- **Informational atomism:** A concept's meaning is grounded in law-like connections between the world and mental representations.
- Fodor's position implies that most concepts are innate rather than constructed through definitions or combinations of simpler concepts.

**NET keyword:** Conceptual atomism = concepts are largely unstructured and grounded in mind–world relations.

## **6. Comparison with Other Cognitive Theorists**

| Theorist           | Main contribution                    | Position                   |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------ | -------------------------- |
| **Jerry Fodor**    | LOTH, modularity, conceptual atomism | Classical computationalism |
| **Newell & Simon** | PSSH, problem-space theory           | Symbol processing          |
| **Noam Chomsky**   | Universal Grammar, LAD               | Linguistic nativism        |
| **Paul Smolensky** | Distributed representations          | Connectionism              |

**Important distinction:**

- Fodor's nativism concerns mental representations, concepts and mentalese.
- Chomsky's nativism concerns the innate structure underlying language acquisition.
- Fodor and Pylyshyn challenged connectionism over systematicity.

## **7. Major Contributions**

1. **LOTH:** Philosophical foundation for the computational-representational theory of mind.
2. **Modularity:** Distinguished modular input systems from non-modular central systems.
3. **Systematicity:** Strengthened the classical-versus-connectionist debate.
4. **Conceptual atomism:** Offered an alternative to definitional and prototype theories.
5. **Intentional realism:** Defended beliefs and desires as real, causally effective mental states.

## **8. Criticisms**

1. **Modularity:** Evidence of top-down influences on perception challenges strict informational encapsulation.
2. **LOTH:** Mentalese is difficult to observe or test directly.
3. **Conceptual atomism:** The claim that most concepts are innate and unstructured is controversial.
4. **Connectionism:** Connectionist models propose distributed representations as an alternative to classical symbolic architecture.
5. **Frame problem:** Fodor later argued that both classical computationalism and connectionism struggle to explain how cognition selects relevant information for a particular situation.
