## **1. Introduction & Background**

- **Allan M. Collins** and **Elizabeth F. Loftus** proposed the **Spreading Activation Theory of Semantic Processing** in 1975.
- It developed as an alternative to the rigid hierarchical structure proposed by Collins and Quillian (1969).
- Their theory explains how activating one concept makes related concepts easier to retrieve.
- **Key publication:** _A Spreading-Activation Theory of Semantic Processing_ (1975), published in _Psychological Review_, 82(6), 407–428.
- The theory is particularly important for explaining **semantic priming, typicality effects and associative relationships** in semantic memory.

**NET association:** Collins and Loftus (1975) = Spreading Activation Theory.

## **2. Core Philosophy — Spreading Activation**

The central proposition is that semantic memory consists of interconnected concepts, and activation of one concept spreads to other related concepts.

### **Key concepts**

**1. Nodes**

- Represent individual concepts or pieces of semantic information.
- Example: Lion, Tiger, Animal and Forest.

**2. Links**

- Connect related concepts.
- The strength of connections reflects the degree of semantic association.

**3. Spreading Activation**

- When one concept is activated, some activation spreads through its connections to related concepts.
- Closely associated concepts receive stronger activation.
- This makes related concepts easier and faster to retrieve.

**4. Semantic Distance**

- Represents the degree of relatedness between concepts.
- Closely related concepts have shorter semantic distances.
- More distant concepts have weaker associations.

**5. Priming**

- Prior activation of a concept facilitates the processing of a related concept.
- This is known as semantic priming.

**NET association:** Semantic distance = Degree of conceptual relatedness, rather than simply the number of hierarchical levels.

## **3. Major Assumptions of the Model**

| Assumption           | Explanation                                                                               |
| -------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Network organisation | Concepts are represented as interconnected nodes.                                         |
| Spreading activation | Activation travels from one concept to related concepts.                                  |
| Link strength        | Stronger associations facilitate faster activation.                                       |
| Semantic distance    | Relatedness determines how easily activation spreads.                                     |
| Priming              | Previous activation facilitates subsequent processing of related concepts.                |

## **4. Empirical Phenomena Explained by the Theory**

### **1. Semantic Priming Effect**

- A word is recognised faster when it is preceded by a semantically related word.
- Example:
  - Prime: DOCTOR
  - Target: NURSE
- The word Doctor activates related concepts, including Nurse.
- Consequently, Nurse is processed faster when it appears.

**Important:** Semantic priming is evidence consistent with spreading activation.

### **2. Typicality Effect**

- Typical members of a category are often verified faster than atypical members.
- Example:
  - Robin is a bird.
  - Chicken is a bird.
- Robin is generally considered a more typical bird than chicken.
- Typical category members have stronger associations with the category concept and are therefore accessed more readily.

**NET association:** Typicality effect = A major challenge to the strict hierarchical model of Collins and Quillian.

### **3. Familiarity Effect**

- Familiar and frequently encountered associations are processed more quickly.
- Frequently used connections are assumed to have stronger associative links.
- Stronger links facilitate activation and retrieval.

### **4. Negative Sentence Verification**

- False statements can take different amounts of time to reject.
- Semantic similarity between concepts can influence verification time.
- For example, closely related concepts may create greater interference than unrelated concepts.

## **5. Comparison with Collins & Quillian (1969)**

| Dimension      | Collins & Quillian (1969)            | Collins & Loftus (1975)                       |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------- |
| Model          | Hierarchical Network                 | Spreading Activation                          |
| Organisation   | Strict hierarchy                     | Flexible associative network                  |
| Retrieval      | Traversal of hierarchical links      | Spreading activation                          |
| Main principle | Cognitive economy                    | Semantic distance                             |
| Retrieval time | Number of hierarchical levels        | Semantic relatedness and association strength |
| Typicality     | Difficult to explain                 | Better accommodates typicality                |
| Priming        | Not central to the original account  | Important explanatory mechanism               |
| Main criticism | Cannot adequately explain typicality | Flexibility makes precise testing difficult   |

### **Most Important Difference**

- **Collins and Quillian:** Information is organised hierarchically, with properties stored at the highest applicable level.
- **Collins and Loftus:** Concepts are interconnected through associative links, and activation spreads between related concepts.

**NET trap:** Do not attribute cognitive economy to Collins and Loftus. It is a central principle of Collins and Quillian's original model.

## **6. Criticisms of Spreading Activation Theory**

### **1. Difficult to Falsify**

- The model is highly flexible.
- Different findings can be explained by adjusting associative strengths and semantic distances.
- This makes it difficult to establish precise predictions that could disprove the theory.

Important NET criticism

### **2. Ambiguity of Semantic Distance**

- The theory does not provide a universally precise measurement of semantic distance.
- The strength of associations may vary between individuals and contexts.

### **3. Individual and Cultural Differences**

- People may have different associations based on their experiences, knowledge and cultural backgrounds.
- Therefore, semantic networks may not be identical across individuals.

### **4. Limited Explanation of Neural Mechanisms**

- The model describes semantic processing at a cognitive level.
- It does not specify the precise neural mechanisms responsible for spreading activation.

### **5. Competition from Connectionist Models**

- Later connectionist approaches explained semantic knowledge through distributed patterns of activation rather than relying exclusively on discrete, localised concept nodes.
- These models offered alternative explanations of semantic representation and processing.

## **7. Contributions**

1. Explained how activation of one concept facilitates access to related concepts.
2. Provided an account of semantic priming.
3. Offered a more flexible alternative to strict hierarchical organisation.
4. Helped explain typicality and familiarity effects.
5. Influenced subsequent research on semantic networks and associative memory.
6. Contributed to the development of cognitive explanations of language comprehension and semantic processing.
