# **Hermann Ebbinghaus's Experimental Psychology of Memory — Quick Revision Notes**

**UGC-NET JRF Psychology | December 2026**

## **1. Introduction & Background**

- **Hermann Ebbinghaus (1850–1909)** was a German psychologist and a pioneer of experimental memory research.
- He was among the first psychologists to investigate higher mental processes using systematic laboratory methods.
- Before Ebbinghaus, memory was largely considered difficult to study objectively and experimentally.
- He demonstrated that memory could be measured through controlled experiments and quantitative methods.
- **Key publication:** _Memory: A Contribution to Experimental Psychology_ (1885), originally published in German as _Über das Gedächtnis_.
- His major contributions include the **forgetting curve, method of savings, nonsense syllables, spacing effect and overlearning**.

**NET association:** Ebbinghaus (1885) = Experimental study of memory.

## **2. Core Philosophy — Memory Can Be Measured Scientifically**

Ebbinghaus's central proposition was that memory is an experimentally measurable psychological process.

### **Major methodological innovations**

**1. Nonsense Syllables**

- Ebbinghaus developed meaningless consonant-vowel-consonant (CVC) combinations.
- Examples: ZUK, BAF, DAX.
- These were used to minimise the influence of prior knowledge, meaning and personal associations.
- This allowed him to study relatively controlled forms of learning and retention.

**2. Self-Experimentation**

- Ebbinghaus conducted his major memory experiments on himself.
- He carefully controlled learning conditions and recorded learning and relearning performance.

**3. Quantitative Measurement**

- He measured learning through repetition, time and relearning performance.
- His approach established a foundation for experimental investigations of memory.

**NET association:** Nonsense syllables = Minimising the influence of meaning and previous associations.

## **3. Method of Savings — Most Important Contribution**

The **Method of Savings** was Ebbinghaus's major technique for measuring memory retention.

### **Definition**

It measures how much learning is preserved by calculating the amount of effort saved when previously learned material is relearned.

### **Procedure**

1. The participant learns a list of nonsense syllables until a predetermined criterion is achieved.
2. A retention interval is introduced.
3. The participant relearns the same material.
4. The original learning effort is compared with the relearning effort.

### **Formula**

\\[ \text{Savings (\\%)} = \frac{\text{Original learning time}-\text{Relearning time}} {\text{Original learning time}}\times100 \\]

### **Example**

Suppose:

- Original learning time = 100 minutes.
- Relearning time = 40 minutes.

\\[ \text{Savings}=\frac{100-40}{100}\times100 \\]

\\[ =60\\% \\]

60% savings

This indicates that 60% of the original learning effort was saved during relearning.

### **Importance**

- Demonstrated that memory can remain even when conscious recall is difficult.
- Provided an indirect measure of retention.
- Helped reveal that forgetting does not necessarily mean complete loss of previously learned information.

**NET trap:** Ebbinghaus measured retention through savings, not exclusively through recall or recognition.

## **4. The Forgetting Curve — Most Famous Discovery**

The **Forgetting Curve** describes how the amount of retained information decreases over time.

### **Main finding**

Forgetting is initially rapid and subsequently becomes slower.

### **Important characteristics**

1. **Rapid initial forgetting:** A substantial amount of information is lost shortly after learning.
2. **Gradual decline:** The rate of forgetting decreases over time.
3. **Relative stabilisation:** The remaining retained information becomes more stable over longer intervals.
4. **Nonlinear pattern:** Forgetting does not occur at a constant rate.

### **Approximate retention figures**

The following are commonly reproduced approximate values from Ebbinghaus's original experiments:

| Retention interval | Approximate retention |
| ------------------ | --------------------- |
| 20 minutes         | 58%                   |
| 1 hour             | 44%                   |
| 9 hours            | 36%                   |
| 1 day              | 34%                   |
| 6 days             | 23%                   |
| 31 days            | 21%                   |

These values should be treated as illustrative experimental estimates, not universal forgetting rates.

### **Shape of the Forgetting Curve**

Remember this pattern

- Initially steep decline.
- Gradually becomes flatter.
- Retention decreases more slowly as time passes.

### **Mathematical description**

Ebbinghaus's forgetting data have been described using logarithmic and power-function relationships. Modern analyses commonly find power functions useful for modelling forgetting.

**NET association:** Forgetting curve = Rapid initial forgetting followed by a gradual slowing of forgetting.

## **5. Spacing Effect — Distributed vs. Massed Practice**

The **Spacing Effect** refers to the finding that distributing learning sessions over time generally produces better long-term retention than concentrating the same practice into one session.

### **Two types of practice**

| Distributed Practice                         | Massed Practice                             |
| -------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| Learning is divided across multiple sessions | Learning occurs in one concentrated session |
| Repetitions are separated by intervals       | Repetitions occur close together            |
| Generally improves long-term retention       | Often produces poorer long-term retention   |
| Also called spaced practice                  | Commonly associated with cramming           |

### **Ebbinghaus's contribution**

- Ebbinghaus documented the advantages of distributing repetitions across time.
- He observed that an appropriate distribution of repetitions was more beneficial than massing them together.
- Later researchers, including Jost (1897), further investigated the spacing phenomenon.

### **Jost's Law (1897)**

Jost's Law is an important related concept:

- When two memory traces are of equal strength, the older trace benefits more from additional practice than the newer trace.
- This helps explain why spacing and repeated practice can be particularly beneficial for older memories.

**NET association:** Ebbinghaus = Early experimental demonstration of spacing; Jost = Jost's Law.

## **6. Other Important Findings**

### **1. Overlearning**

- Overlearning means continuing to practise material even after the required learning criterion has been achieved.
- Additional repetitions can improve retention and resistance to forgetting.

**Example:** A student continues revising a list even after being able to reproduce it correctly.

### **2. List-Length Effect**

- Longer lists generally require more learning time than shorter lists.
- Increasing the number of items increases the amount of material that must be mastered.

### **3. Meaningful Material**

- Meaningful material is generally learned more easily than meaningless material.
- Ebbinghaus compared the learning of nonsense syllables with meaningful material, including poetry.
- Meaningful associations facilitate learning.

### **4. Savings and Relearning**

- Relearning can be faster even when the learner cannot fully recall the original material.
- This demonstrates the difference between accessible conscious recall and retained learning.

### **5. Ebbinghaus Illusion**

- Ebbinghaus also contributed to visual perception research.
- The Ebbinghaus illusion is a size-contrast illusion.
- A central circle appears larger or smaller depending on the size of the surrounding circles.

**Important distinction:** The Ebbinghaus illusion is related to visual perception, not his experimental theory of memory.

## **7. Contributions and Limitations**

### **Contributions**

1. Established experimental memory research as a scientific field.
2. Introduced nonsense syllables for controlled memory experiments.
3. Developed the method of savings.
4. Demonstrated the nonlinear nature of forgetting.
5. Documented the benefits of distributed practice.
6. Investigated overlearning and the effects of meaningfulness.
7. Influenced subsequent experimental research on learning, retention and forgetting.

### **Limitations**

1. **Limited generalisability:** Much of his original research was conducted on himself.
2. **Artificial material:** Nonsense syllables do not fully represent everyday meaningful learning.
3. **Individual differences:** His findings may not apply equally to all individuals and learning situations.
4. **Limited ecological validity:** Laboratory learning differs from complex real-world learning.
5. **Forgetting is not always simple decay:** Interference, retrieval failure and contextual changes can also contribute to forgetting.
