## **1. Introduction & Background**

- **Eleanor Rosch (born 1938)** is an American cognitive psychologist known for her influential research on human categorization and concept formation.
- She challenged the traditional classical view that categories are defined by necessary and sufficient features.
- She proposed that categories have an **internal structure**, in which some members are more representative than others.
- Her research transformed the understanding of how people organise, recognise and classify objects.

## **2. Core Philosophy — Categories Have Graded Membership**

Rosch argued that categories are not necessarily rigid groups in which every member has equal status. Instead, category membership is often **graded**, meaning that some members are more representative than others.

### **Key principles**

**1. Graded Membership**

- Members of a category differ in their degree of representativeness.
- Example: A robin is generally considered a more typical bird than a penguin.
- Both belong to the category Bird, but they are not equally representative.

**2. Prototype**

- A prototype represents the most typical or representative example of a category.
- It reflects the features commonly associated with category membership.
- Example: A robin may serve as a prototypical example of a bird.

**3. Fuzzy Category Boundaries**

- Categories do not always have sharply defined boundaries.
- Some objects are clearly identifiable members, while others are less typical or borderline cases.

**4. Family Resemblance**

- Members of a category share overlapping characteristics.
- They need not all possess one identical feature.
- Different members may resemble one another in different ways.

**NET association:** Prototype Theory = Graded membership rather than all-or-none category membership.

## **3. Three Levels of Categorization — Highly Important**

Rosch identified three levels of categorization:

1. Superordinate level
2. Basic level
3. Subordinate level

| Level             | Example       | Characteristics                                                  |
| ----------------- | ------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Superordinate** | Furniture     | Broad category; members share relatively few specific attributes |
| **Basic**         | Chair         | Most useful and cognitively efficient level of categorization    |
| **Subordinate**   | Kitchen chair | More specific category with additional distinguishing features   |

### **Why is the Basic Level Privileged?**

The basic level is considered the most cognitively useful level because:

- Objects are generally categorised quickly at this level.
- It is often acquired early during language development.
- It provides useful information without requiring excessive detail.
- Objects at this level often share a common perceptual appearance.
- They frequently share a common motor interaction or motor programme.

### **Example**

Consider the following categorisation:

| Superordinate | Basic | Subordinate        |
| ------------- | ----- | ------------------ |
| Animal        | Dog   | German Shepherd    |
| Furniture     | Chair | Dining chair       |
| Vehicle       | Car   | Sedan              |
| Fruit         | Apple | Granny Smith apple |

Most important NET concept

**Basic level = The most cognitively efficient level of categorization.**

**NET trap:** Do not confuse the basic level with the most specific level. The subordinate level is more specific.

## **4. Family Resemblance Principle — Rosch & Mervis (1975)**

The **Family Resemblance Principle** was developed in the influential research of Rosch and Mervis (1975).

### **Definition**

Category members are related through overlapping similarities rather than necessarily sharing one common defining characteristic.

### **Main features**

- Prototypical members share many attributes with other members of their category.
- They share relatively few attributes with members of contrasting categories.
- Typicality depends partly on the distribution of attributes across category members.
- Categories are organised around patterns of similarity.

### **Example: Category Bird**

| Member  | Typicality | Explanation                                                       |
| ------- | ---------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Robin   | High       | Strongly represents commonly associated bird characteristics      |
| Sparrow | High       | Shares many typical bird attributes                               |
| Duck    | Moderate   | Has some distinctive characteristics                              |
| Penguin | Low        | Lacks some commonly expected bird characteristics, such as flight |

**Important:** Atypical members are still members of the category. A penguin does not stop being a bird because it cannot fly.

### **Cue Validity**

Cue validity refers to how strongly a particular attribute predicts membership in a category.

Rosch and Mervis used the distribution of attributes across category members to explain why some members are more prototypical than others.

**NET association:** Rosch and Mervis (1975) = Family Resemblance and prototypicality.

## **5. Empirical Evidence for Prototype Theory**

### **1. Typicality Effect**

- Typical category members are generally verified faster than atypical members.
- Example:
  - "A robin is a bird."
  - "A penguin is a bird."
- The first statement is generally verified faster because robin is more representative of the category Bird.

### **2. Category Listing**

- When people are asked to name examples of a category, they tend to mention typical members first.
- For example, when asked to name birds, people may mention robin, sparrow or pigeon before penguin.

### **3. Priming Effect**

- Activating a category representation can facilitate judgments about typical members.
- Typical members are more closely associated with the category's prototype.

### **4. Developmental Evidence**

- Children often learn and use basic-level category labels early.
- For example, they may learn the word "dog" before more specific labels such as "German Shepherd".

**NET association:** Robin versus penguin = Typicality effect.

## **6. Comparison with Other Theories of Categorization**

| Feature             | Classical Theory                                        | Prototype Theory                       | Exemplar Theory                                 |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| Main idea           | Categories defined by necessary and sufficient features | Categories organised around prototypes | Categories represented by individual examples   |
| Membership          | All-or-none                                             | Graded                                 | Based on similarity to stored examples          |
| Category boundaries | Sharp                                                   | Often fuzzy                            | Often fuzzy                                     |
| Representation      | Defining features                                       | Prototype or central tendency          | Multiple stored exemplars                       |
| Typicality          | Not adequately explained                                | Central concept                        | Explained through similarity to stored examples |

### **Classical Theory**

- Categories are defined through necessary and sufficient characteristics.
- Every member must possess the defining features.
- Membership is generally treated as all-or-none.

### **Prototype Theory**

- Categories are represented through typical or central tendencies.
- Some members are more representative than others.
- Membership is graded.

### **Exemplar Theory**

- Categories are represented through memories of individual examples.
- A new object is classified by comparing it with stored examples.
- Unlike prototype theory, it does not require a single abstract prototype.

**NET trap:** Prototype Theory = Typical representation; Exemplar Theory = Stored individual examples.

## **7. Criticisms of Prototype Theory**

### **1. Circularity Problem**

- The theory explains typicality through similarity to a prototype.
- However, it does not fully explain why particular features become important in determining the prototype.
- This creates a potential circularity problem.

### **2. Difficulty Representing Variability**

- A prototype represents central tendencies.
- It may fail to capture the full range of variation within a category.
- For example, apples vary greatly in size, colour and shape.

### **3. Ignoring Causal Relationships**

- Categorisation may depend on causal and functional relationships, not just similarity.
- Two objects can look similar but belong to different categories because of their underlying nature or function.

### **4. Prototype Ambiguity**

\- It is not always clear whether a prototype is:

- An actual representative example.
- An abstract average of category members.
- A mental representation of typical features.

### **5. Contextual Influences**

- Typicality may change depending on the situation.
- For example, the most representative bird in a pet shop may differ from the most representative bird in a wildlife context.

**NET association:** Circularity and difficulty representing within-category variability are important criticisms.

## **8. Contributions**

1. Challenged the traditional classical theory of categorization.
2. Demonstrated that categories have internal structures.
3. Established graded membership and typicality as important concepts.
4. Explained the importance of basic-level categorization.
5. Developed the family resemblance approach with Mervis.
6. Influenced cognitive psychology, linguistics, anthropology and cognitive science.
7. Improved understanding of concept formation and natural categories.
