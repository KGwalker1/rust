## **1. Introduction & Background**

- **Daniel Kahneman (1934–2024):** Israeli-American psychologist; major contributor to cognitive psychology, judgment and decision-making, and behavioural economics.
- **Major achievement:** Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences (2002), shared with Vernon Smith, for integrating psychological research into economic science.
- **Key works:**
  - _Attention and Effort_ (1973) – Capacity Model of Attention.
  - _Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases_ (1974, with Amos Tversky).
  - _Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under Risk_ (1979, with Amos Tversky).
  - _Thinking, Fast and Slow_ (2011) – Popularised System 1 and System 2 thinking.

**NET focus:** Kahneman = Capacity Model + Heuristics and Biases + Prospect Theory + Dual-Process Thinking.

## **2. Cognitive Miser and Bounded Rationality**

- **Cognitive Miser:** Humans have limited cognitive resources and often use mental shortcuts to simplify complex decisions.
- **Heuristics:** Simple mental strategies used to make judgments and decisions quickly.
- **Cognitive Biases:** Systematic errors in judgment that may arise from using heuristics.
- **Bounded Rationality:** Human rationality is limited by cognitive capacity, available information and time.

**Important distinction:**

- Heuristics = Mental shortcuts.
- Biases = Systematic errors in judgment.
- Bounded rationality = Limits of human decision-making.

**NET pointer:** Kahneman and Tversky demonstrated that human judgments frequently deviate from normative statistical and logical principles.

## **3. Attention and Effort (1973) – Capacity Model of Attention**

### **Core Concept**

Kahneman proposed that attention is a **limited pool of mental resources** that can be flexibly distributed among different tasks.

### **Major Assumptions**

1. **Limited capacity:** Humans have a finite amount of attentional resources.
2. **Flexible allocation:** Resources can be distributed among multiple activities.
3. **Arousal:** The availability of attentional resources is influenced by the individual's level of arousal.
4. **Task demands:** Difficult tasks require more attentional resources than simple tasks.
5. **Allocation policy:** Attention is distributed according to current intentions, enduring dispositions and task demands.

### **Pupil Dilation and Mental Effort**

- Kahneman's research demonstrated that pupil dilation is associated with mental effort.
- Greater cognitive effort is generally associated with increased pupil diameter.
- Pupil dilation can therefore serve as a physiological indicator of cognitive processing demands.

**NET pointer:** Kahneman's model = Limited-capacity resource allocation, NOT Broadbent's selective filter.

## **4. Heuristics and Biases (1974) – Kahneman & Tversky**

Kahneman and Amos Tversky identified three major heuristics used in human judgment under uncertainty.

### **A. Representativeness Heuristic**

- Judging the probability of an event based on how closely it resembles a typical category or stereotype.
- People often ignore actual statistical probabilities and base decisions on similarity.

**Associated biases:**

- Base-rate neglect.
- Gambler's fallacy.
- Law of small numbers.

**Example:** Assuming someone is a librarian because they are quiet and organised, without considering the actual proportion of librarians in the population.

### **B. Availability Heuristic**

- Judging the probability or frequency of an event based on how easily examples come to mind.
- Vivid, recent or emotionally significant events are more easily recalled.

**Example:** Overestimating the probability of an airplane accident after watching extensive news coverage of a crash.

### **C. Anchoring and Adjustment Heuristic**

- People rely heavily on an initial value or piece of information (anchor) when making judgments.
- They subsequently adjust their estimates, but the adjustment is usually insufficient.

**Example:** An initially suggested price influences how much a person considers reasonable for a product.

### **Comparison of Three Heuristics**

| Heuristic          | Basis of judgment        | Associated bias             |
| ------------------ | ------------------------ | --------------------------- |
| Representativeness | Similarity or stereotype | Base-rate neglect           |
| Availability       | Ease of recall           | Frequency estimation errors |
| Anchoring          | Initial information      | Insufficient adjustment     |

### **Law of Small Numbers**

- People incorrectly assume that small samples accurately represent the characteristics of the entire population.
- They expect small samples to display greater representativeness than statistical probability actually guarantees.
- This can produce the **Gambler's Fallacy**.

**Example:** After observing five consecutive heads in a coin toss, someone believes tails must occur next to balance the outcomes.

**NET pointer:**

- Similarity = Representativeness.
- Recall = Availability.
- Initial value = Anchoring.
- Gambler's fallacy = Representativeness.

## **5. Prospect Theory (1979) – Kahneman & Tversky**

Prospect Theory was proposed as an alternative to the traditional **Expected Utility Theory** of economic decision-making.

### **Major Principles**

**A. Reference Dependence**

- Individuals evaluate gains and losses relative to a reference point rather than absolute wealth.
- The reference point is often the current status quo.

**B. Loss Aversion**

- Losses have a greater psychological impact than equivalent gains.
- Losing ₹1,000 generally feels more painful than gaining ₹1,000 feels pleasurable.

**C. Diminishing Sensitivity**

- The psychological difference between two outcomes decreases as their magnitude increases.
- The difference between gaining ₹100 and ₹200 feels greater than the difference between gaining ₹1,100 and ₹1,200.

**D. Probability Weighting**

\- People tend to overweight small probabilities and underweight moderate and high probabilities.

## **6. System 1 and System 2 Thinking (2011)**

Popularised by Kahneman in _Thinking, Fast and Slow_.

| System 1              | System 2                         |
| --------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| Fast thinking         | Slow thinking                    |
| Automatic             | Controlled                       |
| Intuitive             | Analytical                       |
| Effortless            | Effortful                        |
| Associative           | Rule-governed                    |
| Often unconscious     | Conscious and deliberate         |
| Generates impressions | Monitors and evaluates responses |

### **Interaction Between the Two Systems**

- System 1 continuously generates impressions, intuitions and suggestions.
- System 2 monitors and evaluates these suggestions.
- System 2 usually accepts System 1's responses unless additional effort is required.
- When a difficult problem arises, System 2 becomes more actively involved.

**Example:**

- Recognising a familiar face = System 1.
- Solving a complicated mathematical problem = System 2.

**NET pointer:** System 1 = Fast, automatic and intuitive; System 2 = Slow, controlled and analytical.

### **Important Distinction: Kahneman vs Simon**

- **Herbert Simon:** Introduced bounded rationality and satisficing.
- **Daniel Kahneman:** Demonstrated systematic judgmental biases through heuristics and developed Prospect Theory with Tversky.

## **8. Major Contributions**

1. **Capacity Model of Attention (1973):** Established attention as a limited pool of mental resources.
2. **Heuristics and Biases (1974):** Demonstrated systematic errors in human judgment.
3. **Prospect Theory (1979):** Explained decision-making under risk through reference dependence and loss aversion.
4. **Dual-Process Thinking:** Popularised the distinction between intuitive and analytical thinking.
5. **Behavioural Economics:** Helped integrate psychological findings into economic theories.
6. **Nobel Prize (2002):** Recognition for integrating psychological research into economic science.

## **9. Criticisms**

1. **Overemphasis on irrationality:** Critics argue that heuristics can be adaptive and effective in real-world environments.
2. **Ecological validity:** Laboratory decision-making tasks may not fully represent complex real-life decisions.
3. **Replication concerns:** Some findings associated with the broader judgment and self-control research tradition have faced replication challenges.
4. **Prospect Theory limitations:** The original model was developed primarily to explain decisions involving risk with known probabilities.
5. **Dual-process oversimplification:** System 1 and System 2 are useful explanatory categories, but actual cognitive processes are more complex than two separate systems.
