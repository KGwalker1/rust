# **Fergus Craik & Robert Lockhart's Levels of Processing (LOP) Framework — Quick Revision Notes**

## **1. Introduction & Background**

- **Fergus I. M. Craik (born 1935)** and **Robert S. Lockhart (born 1939)** proposed the **Levels of Processing (LOP) Framework** in **1972**.
- **Key publication:** _Levels of Processing: A Framework for Memory Research_ (1972), published in the _Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior_.
- The framework challenged traditional store-based explanations of memory, particularly Atkinson and Shiffrin's Multi-Store Model.
- It shifted attention from **where information is stored** to **how information is processed**.
- It proposed that the durability of memory depends largely on the depth of cognitive processing.

**NET association:** Craik and Lockhart (1972) = Levels of Processing Framework.

## **2. Core Philosophy — Memory as Processing, Not Storage**

The central proposition is that memory retention depends on the **depth of processing** performed on information rather than simply the memory store in which it is located.

### **Key propositions**

1. **Process over structure:** Memory should be understood through cognitive processes rather than separate structural stores.
2. **Depth determines retention:** Deeper processing generally produces stronger and more durable memory.
3. **Memory as a by-product:** Memory traces develop as a consequence of perceptual and cognitive processing.
4. **Meaning matters:** Semantic processing generally produces better retention than processing only physical or sound characteristics.
5. **STM reconsidered:** Short-term memory is viewed more as continuing mental activity than as a completely separate structural store.

**NET trap:** LOP is a _processing framework_, not a three-store memory model.

## **3. Shallow and Deep Processing**

Craik and Lockhart described processing as a hierarchy, progressing from relatively shallow analysis to deeper semantic analysis.

### **Three levels of processing**

| Level          | Type of processing            | Example                                            | Depth        |
| -------------- | ----------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- | ------------ |
| **Structural** | Physical appearance of a word | Is the word written in capital letters?            | Shallow      |
| **Phonemic**   | Sound of a word               | Does the word rhyme with "CAT"?                    | Intermediate |
| **Semantic**   | Meaning of a word             | Does the word fit meaningfully into this sentence? | Deep         |

### **A. Structural Processing**

- Focuses on the physical characteristics of information.
- Examines features such as shape, font and capitalisation.
- Generally produces relatively weak memory traces.

### **B. Phonemic Processing**

- Focuses on the sound or pronunciation of information.
- Involves identifying rhymes and phonological characteristics.
- Deeper than structural processing, but generally shallower than semantic processing.

### **C. Semantic Processing**

- Focuses on meaning and interpretation.
- Involves connecting information with existing knowledge.
- Generally produces stronger and more durable memory traces.

**Hierarchy of Processing**

**Structural Processing**

Physical appearance — shallow

**Phonemic Processing**

Sound — intermediate

**Semantic Processing**

Meaning — deep; generally better retention

### **Craik & Tulving's Experiment (1975)**

- Participants were presented with words and asked questions requiring different levels of processing.
- Structural questions examined whether words were printed in capital letters.
- Phonemic questions examined whether words rhymed with another word.
- Semantic questions examined whether words fitted meaningfully into sentences.
- **Finding:** Recognition and recall were generally better following semantic processing than phonemic or structural processing.

**NET association:** Craik & Tulving (1975) = Experimental evidence for Levels of Processing.

## **4. Two Types of Rehearsal**

Craik and Lockhart distinguished between two forms of rehearsal.

| Maintenance Rehearsal                               | Elaborative Rehearsal                            |
| --------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| Repetition of information                           | Meaningful elaboration of information            |
| Maintains information at a similar processing level | Involves deeper processing                       |
| Example: Repeating a phone number                   | Connecting a new concept with existing knowledge |
| Does not necessarily produce durable LTM            | Generally supports better long-term retention    |

### **Maintenance Rehearsal**

- Involves repeating information without adding meaningful associations.
- Helps maintain information temporarily.
- Example: Repeating a date several times without understanding its significance.

### **Elaborative Rehearsal**

- Involves connecting new information with existing knowledge.
- Creates meaningful associations.
- Example: Remembering a psychological theory by relating it to a real-life example.

**NET trap:** Repetition alone does not guarantee long-term retention. The quality and depth of processing matter.

## **5. Comparison with Atkinson & Shiffrin's Multi-Store Model**

| Dimension          | Atkinson & Shiffrin (1968)               | Craik & Lockhart (1972)              |
| ------------------ | ---------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| Main approach      | Structural model                         | Process-oriented framework           |
| Main focus         | Memory stores                            | Depth of processing                  |
| Memory explanation | Sensory, STM and LTM                     | Shallow-to-deep processing           |
| STM                | Separate structural store                | Continuing activity                  |
| Rehearsal          | Important control process for transfer   | Quality of processing matters        |
| Best retention     | Associated with transfer to LTM          | Associated with deeper processing    |
| Key evidence       | Serial-position and memory-store studies | Craik & Tulving's (1975) experiments |

**Remember:** Atkinson and Shiffrin = _Where information is stored_; Craik and Lockhart = _How information is processed_.

## **6. Criticisms of the Levels of Processing Framework**

### **1. Circularity Problem**

- The framework does not provide an independent, objective measure of processing depth.
- Deep processing is often inferred from better memory performance.
- Better memory is then explained by deep processing.
- This creates a circular explanation.

Most important criticism for NET

### **2. Difficulty Measuring Depth**

- It is difficult to determine precisely how deep a particular cognitive process is.
- The framework does not provide a clear measurement scale for processing depth.

### **3. Lack of Clear Processing Boundaries**

- It does not adequately explain the different levels within broad categories such as semantic processing.
- Not all semantic tasks involve the same degree of elaboration.

### **4. Rote Rehearsal Can Support Learning**

- Repetition is not always ineffective.
- Under certain conditions, rote rehearsal can contribute to learning and retention.

### **5. Shallow Processing Can Sometimes Produce Durable Memories**

- Some shallowly processed information may be remembered well.
- Therefore, shallow processing does not invariably produce weak memory traces.

### **6. Retrieval Processes Are Neglected**

- The framework concentrates heavily on encoding.
- It gives comparatively less attention to retrieval processes and retrieval cues.

**NET association:** The circularity problem is the classic criticism of the LOP framework.

## **7. Contributions**

1. Shifted memory research from structural stores to cognitive processing.
2. Highlighted the importance of semantic processing in memory retention.
3. Distinguished maintenance rehearsal from elaborative rehearsal.
4. Influenced subsequent research into encoding, learning and memory.
5. Helped establish the importance of meaningful learning strategies in education.
