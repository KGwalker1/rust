# **Irving Biederman's Recognition-by-Components (RBC) Theory — Quick Revision Notes**

## **1. Introduction & Background**

- **Irving Biederman (born 1939):** American cognitive psychologist and neuroscientist known for his work on object recognition and visual perception.
- **Major contribution:** Developed the **Recognition-by-Components (RBC) Theory**, published in 1987.
- **Key publication:** _Recognition-by-components: A theory of human image understanding_ (1987), published in _Psychological Review_.
- His theory explains how humans recognise objects by decomposing their visual structure into basic three-dimensional geometric components called **geons**.

**NET association:** Biederman = RBC Theory + Geons + Non-accidental properties (NAPs).

## **2. Core Philosophy — Objects as Arrangements of Geons**

Biederman proposed that object recognition occurs through the identification of simple geometric components and their spatial relationships.

Key propositions:

1. **Component-based recognition:** Objects are decomposed into simpler geometric components.
2. **Geons:** Approximately **36 basic geometric components** are proposed to be sufficient for representing many everyday objects.
3. **Structural descriptions:** Objects are recognised through the arrangement and relationships of their geons.
4. **Viewpoint invariance:** Recognition can remain relatively stable across different viewing angles because geons are derived from relatively stable structural properties.
5. **Robust recognition:** Objects can often be recognised even when portions of their contours are missing or the image is degraded.

**NET memory trick:** RBC = Recognising objects through their Components.

- **Geons** are basic three-dimensional volumetric components used to construct mental representations of objects.
- The term refers to _geometric ions_.
- They are based on simple volumetric forms, such as cylinders, blocks and curved components.
- Complex objects can be represented through combinations of these components.

### **Non-Accidental Properties (NAPs)**

Geons are recovered from structural properties of image contours that are unlikely to arise accidentally from a particular viewing angle.

Important properties include:

| NAP               | Meaning                                        |
| ----------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| **Straightness**  | Whether an edge is straight or curved          |
| **Parallelism**   | Whether edges are parallel or non-parallel     |
| **Cotermination** | Whether edges meet at a common point           |
| **Symmetry**      | Whether a structure has symmetrical properties |
| **Co-linearity**  | Whether edges lie along the same straight line |

**Why are they called non-accidental?**

These properties are unlikely to occur by chance from a particular viewpoint. They therefore provide useful information about the underlying three-dimensional structure of objects.

**NET trap:** Geons are derived from NAPs, not from colour or surface texture alone.

## **4. Empirical Evidence — Contour Deletion Studies**

### **Biederman and Cooper (1991)**

- Investigated object recognition using images with portions of their contours deleted.
- Found that objects could remain recognisable even when substantial portions of contours were removed.
- Recognition was possible when the remaining contours still provided sufficient information to recover the geons.

**Conclusion:** Object recognition does not depend exclusively on matching every individual pixel of an image. Structural information is important.

### **Pigeon Studies**

- Research by Van Hamme, Wasserman and Biederman (1992) examined pigeons' discrimination of object drawings.
- Pigeons performed relatively well with complementary contour images.
- Performance decreased when contours were spatially scrambled, disrupting the structural information needed for geon recovery.

**NET association:** Contour deletion → Geon recovery → Structural object recognition.

## **5. Biederman's Contribution to Scene Perception**

Biederman also investigated how people recognise objects within meaningful visual scenes.

### **Biederman, Mezzanotte and Rabinowitz (1982)**

- Demonstrated that scene context influences object recognition.
- Objects that are inconsistent with their surroundings can be harder to recognise.
- For example, an object appearing in an unusual location may be detected more slowly.

### **Biederman, Rabinowitz, Glass and Stacy (1974)**

- Investigated how quickly people extract semantic information from visual scenes.
- Their research demonstrated that observers can obtain meaningful information from scenes very rapidly.

**NET association:** Biederman → Object recognition through geons + Influence of scene context.

## **6. Comparison with Other Object Recognition Theorists**

| Dimension      | Biederman                 | Marr                           | Gibson                    |
| -------------- | ------------------------- | ------------------------------ | ------------------------- |
| Theory         | Recognition-by-Components | Computational theory of vision | Ecological theory         |
| Main concept   | Geons                     | Visual representations         | Affordances               |
| Main emphasis  | Structural components     | Information processing         | Environmental information |
| Representation | Geon arrangements         | Primal sketch, 2½-D, 3-D       | Ambient optic array       |
| Approach       | Component-based           | Computational                  | Direct/ecological         |

**Important distinction:** Marr developed a broad computational framework for visual processing. Biederman proposed a more specific account of how objects are represented and recognised through geons.

## **7. Contributions**

1. **RBC Theory:** Provided an influential structural explanation of human object recognition.
2. **Geons:** Proposed basic geometric components for representing complex objects.
3. **Non-accidental properties:** Explained how stable contour properties help recover three-dimensional structure.
4. **Contour deletion research:** Demonstrated the importance of structural information in object recognition.
5. **Scene perception:** Established the importance of contextual relationships in recognising objects.

## **8. Criticisms**

1. **Faces:** Geons alone may not adequately explain fine-grained recognition of faces and other highly complex objects.
2. **Viewpoint dependence:** Some objects are recognised more easily from familiar viewpoints, challenging strong claims of viewpoint invariance.
3. **Limited number of primitives:** The proposed set of approximately 36 geons may not be sufficient to explain every type of object recognition.
4. **Midsegment information:** Recognition may also depend on contour information between vertices, not only on the recovery of geons.
5. **Fine-grained recognition:** RBC explains basic-level recognition more effectively than distinguishing between highly similar objects within the same category.
