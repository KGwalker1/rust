## **1. Introduction to Theories of Emotion**

Emotion is a complex psychological state involving three major components:

1. **Physiological component:** Changes in heart rate, respiration, sweating, hormonal activity, etc.
2. **Cognitive component:** Interpretation and appraisal of the situation.
3. **Behavioural/expressive component:** Facial expressions, gestures, posture and action tendencies.

Different psychologists have proposed different explanations of how these components interact to produce emotional experiences.

The major theories include:

- James–Lange Theory – Physiological changes precede emotional experience.
- Cannon–Bard Theory – Physiological arousal and emotional experience occur simultaneously.
- Schachter–Singer Theory – Emotion results from physiological arousal and cognitive labelling.
- Lazarus's Cognitive-Mediational Theory – Cognitive appraisal precedes and determines emotional experience.
- Lindsley's Activation Theory – Emotional experience is related to the level of physiological and cortical arousal.

## **2. James–Lange Theory of Emotion (1884–1885)**

**Proponents:**

- William James (1884)
- Carl Georg Lange (1885)

**Core idea:** We experience emotion because we perceive bodily physiological changes.

### **A. Basic Assumption**

According to the James–Lange theory, emotional experience occurs as a consequence of physiological changes in the body.

In other words:

**We do not cry because we are sad; we feel sad because we cry.**

Similarly:

- We do not tremble because we are afraid; we feel afraid because we tremble.
- We do not run because we are frightened; we experience fear after perceiving the bodily changes associated with running and defensive reactions.

### **B. Sequence of Emotional Experience**

**Emotional stimulus**

Example: Seeing a snake

**Physiological arousal**

Increased heart rate, sweating, trembling

**Perception of bodily changes**

**Emotional experience**

Feeling of fear

### **C. Important Contributions**

- Emotion is the conscious perception of bodily changes.
- Physiological responses are essential for emotional experience.
- Different emotions are associated with different patterns of bodily responses.
- The theory challenged the traditional assumption that emotion directly produces bodily reactions.

### **D. Criticisms**

1. **Walter Cannon's criticism:** Physiological changes are too slow to explain the rapid occurrence of emotional experiences.
2. Different emotions can produce similar physiological responses.
3. Artificially producing bodily changes does not necessarily produce the corresponding emotion.
4. Some emotional experiences can occur even when bodily feedback is reduced.

### **NET Examination Point**

Remember the direction:

**Stimulus → Physiological response → Emotional experience**

## **3. Cannon–Bard Theory of Emotion (1927)**

**Proponents:**

- Walter Bradford Cannon
- Philip Bard

**Core idea:** Physiological arousal and emotional experience occur simultaneously.

### **A. Background**

Cannon proposed criticisms of the James–Lange theory. Later, Philip Bard developed the theory further.

The theory is also known as the **Thalamic Theory of Emotion**.

### **B. Basic Assumptions**

According to Cannon–Bard, when an individual encounters an emotional stimulus, the brain processes it and produces two simultaneous responses:

1. Physiological arousal.
2. Subjective emotional experience.

Neither response is necessarily the cause of the other.

### **C. Sequence of Emotional Experience**

**Emotional stimulus**

Seeing a dangerous animal

**Central nervous system / brain processing**

**Physiological response**

Heart rate increases, sweating

**Emotional experience**

Conscious feeling of fear

Both responses occur simultaneously.

### **D. Role of the Thalamus**

- Cannon emphasized the role of the thalamus in emotional processing.
- Bard's research highlighted the importance of hypothalamic pathways in emotional expression.
- In the classical explanation, sensory information reaches the thalamus, which distributes signals toward the cortex and hypothalamic mechanisms.

### **E. Criticisms**

1. The thalamus alone cannot explain the complexity of emotional experiences.
2. Emotional processing involves several brain structures, including the amygdala, hypothalamus and prefrontal cortex.
3. Cognitive appraisal and interpretation also influence emotional experiences.

### **NET Examination Point**

**Cannon–Bard = Simultaneous physiological arousal and emotional experience.**

The theory is also called the Thalamic Theory.

## **4. Schachter–Singer Two-Factor Theory of Emotion (1962)**

**Proponents:**

- Stanley Schachter
- Jerome E. Singer

**Core idea:** Emotion is produced by physiological arousal and cognitive interpretation.

### **A. Basic Assumptions**

Schachter and Singer proposed that emotion involves two essential factors:

1. **Physiological arousal:** Bodily activation or autonomic nervous system responses.
2. **Cognitive labelling:** Interpretation of the physiological arousal based on the surrounding situation.

The same physiological arousal can be interpreted as different emotions depending on the context.

### **B. Two-Factor Model**

**Emotional situation**

**Physiological arousal**

Increased heartbeat, sweating

**Cognitive interpretation**

Understanding the situation

**Emotional experience**

### **C. Schachter and Singer's Experiment (1962)**

This is one of the most important experiments for UGC-NET.

**Objective:** To investigate how physiological arousal and cognitive interpretation influence emotional experience.

**Participants:** Male college students.

**Procedure:**

Participants were injected with epinephrine (adrenaline), which produces physiological arousal.

They were divided into different experimental conditions:

| Group    | Condition                                     |
| -------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| Informed | Told about the expected physiological effects |
| Ignorant | Not informed about the effects                |
| Misled   | Given incorrect information about the effects |
| Placebo  | Received a saline injection                   |

Participants were then exposed to a confederate who behaved in either an euphoric or angry manner.

**Findings:**

- Participants who lacked an adequate explanation for their arousal were more likely to interpret it using situational cues.
- Participants who knew the actual effects of adrenaline were less likely to attribute their arousal to the confederate's behaviour.
- The experiment demonstrated the importance of cognitive interpretation in emotional experience.

### **D. Example**

Imagine your heart is beating rapidly.

- At a dangerous location, you may interpret it as fear.
- Before a romantic meeting, you may interpret it as excitement.
- Before an examination, you may interpret it as anxiety.

The physiological arousal may be similar, but the cognitive interpretation differs.

### **E. Criticisms**

1. Physiological arousal is not always necessary for emotional experience.
2. Some emotions can occur without deliberate cognitive labelling.
3. Different emotions may involve distinct physiological patterns.
4. The original experiment's findings and methodology have been debated.

### **NET Examination Point**

**Schachter–Singer = Two-factor theory = Physiological arousal + Cognitive labelling.**

Remember the 1962 epinephrine experiment.

## **5. Lazarus's Cognitive-Mediational Theory of Emotion (1966, 1982)**

**Proponent:** Richard S. Lazarus.

**Core idea:** Cognitive appraisal of a situation determines the emotional response.

### **A. Basic Assumptions**

Lazarus argued that cognitive appraisal is central to the experience of emotion.

An individual does not respond emotionally to a stimulus simply because it exists. The person first evaluates its significance in relation to personal well-being.

For example:

Two students receive the same examination result.

- One student may feel happy because the result meets their expectations.
- Another may feel disappointed because the result is below their expectations.

The same event produces different emotions because of differences in cognitive appraisal.

### **B. Types of Cognitive Appraisal**

Lazarus identified two major forms of appraisal:

**1. Primary Appraisal**

The individual evaluates whether an event is relevant to personal well-being.

It involves three possible evaluations:

| Type            | Meaning                                                      |
| --------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Irrelevant      | The event has no significant personal consequence            |
| Benign-positive | The event is evaluated positively                            |
| Stressful       | The event is evaluated as potentially harmful or challenging |

Stressful appraisal can involve:

- **Harm/Loss:** Damage that has already occurred.
- **Threat:** Anticipation of possible future harm.
- **Challenge:** Opportunity for growth, mastery or gain.

**2. Secondary Appraisal**

The individual evaluates available coping resources and options for managing the situation.

Questions include:

- What can I do?
- Can I control the situation?
- Do I have the necessary resources?
- Can I cope with the consequences?

### **C. Cognitive-Mediational Sequence**

**Environmental stimulus / Event**

**Primary appraisal**

Is this relevant, beneficial or stressful?

**Secondary appraisal**

What coping resources do I have?

**Emotional response**

**Coping response**

_Note: Lazarus's appraisal process is dynamic and can involve reappraisal; the diagram is a simplified learning sequence._

### **D. Reappraisal**

Reappraisal refers to the continuous reassessment of a situation as new information becomes available.

For example:

A student initially experiences fear before an examination but later feels confident after realising that they have prepared adequately.

### **E. Important Contributions**

- Established the importance of cognitive appraisal in emotional experiences.
- Connected emotion with stress and coping.
- Explained why the same event can produce different emotions in different individuals.
- Emphasised the relationship between person and environment.

### **F. Criticisms**

1. Some emotional responses occur rapidly without conscious appraisal.
2. Emotional responses can sometimes precede deliberate cognitive interpretation.
3. It may underestimate automatic and biological contributions to emotion.

### **NET Examination Point**

Remember the distinction:

- Primary appraisal = Significance of the event.
- Secondary appraisal = Coping resources and options.
- Reappraisal = Re-evaluation of the situation.

**Lazarus is particularly important in the psychology of stress and coping.**

## **6. Lindsley's Activation Theory of Emotion (1951)**

**Proponent:** Donald B. Lindsley.

**Core idea:** Emotional behaviour and experience are related to the level of activation or arousal of the central nervous system.

### **A. Background**

Donald B. Lindsley proposed an activation theory that linked emotional behaviour with the physiological activation of the brain.

His work was influenced by developments in neurophysiology, particularly research on the reticular activating system and electroencephalography (EEG).

### **B. Basic Assumptions**

According to the activation perspective:

- The brain has an activation system responsible for maintaining levels of arousal.
- Emotional states are associated with changes in the activation level of the central nervous system.
- The reticular formation plays an important role in regulating cortical arousal.
- Different levels of activation influence alertness, attention and behavioural readiness.

### **C. Role of the Reticular Activating System (RAS)**

The reticular activating system is associated with maintaining wakefulness and regulating cortical activation.

Important functions:

1. Maintains alertness and wakefulness.
2. Regulates levels of cortical arousal.
3. Influences attention and responsiveness.
4. Helps prepare the organism for behavioural responses.

### **D. EEG and Arousal**

Lindsley's activation research connected EEG patterns with different levels of brain activation.

| State               | General EEG pattern                                          |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Alert wakefulness   | Relatively low-amplitude, faster activity                    |
| Relaxed wakefulness | More prominent alpha activity, particularly with eyes closed |
| Drowsiness          | Slower and less regular activity                             |
| Deep sleep          | Prominent slow-wave activity                                 |

The central argument is that changes in brain activation are associated with changes in behavioural and emotional readiness.

### **E. Criticisms**

1. Arousal alone cannot explain the qualitative differences between emotions.
2. Fear, anger and joy may involve similar levels of physiological activation but different subjective experiences.
3. Cognitive appraisal and interpretation are also important in understanding emotion.

### **NET Examination Point**

**Lindsley = Activation theory = CNS arousal + Reticular formation + EEG.**

Do not confuse Lindsley's activation theory with the Yerkes–Dodson law, although both discuss arousal and performance.

### **Common MCQ traps**

1. James–Lange does not propose that emotion causes physiological arousal; it proposes the opposite sequence.
2. Cannon–Bard does not suggest that physiological arousal must occur before emotional experience.
3. Schachter–Singer is called the two-factor theory, not the three-factor theory.
4. Lazarus's primary appraisal concerns the significance of an event, not the resources available to cope.
5. Secondary appraisal concerns coping resources and options.
6. Lindsley's activation theory focuses on the level of CNS activation, not cognitive labelling.
7. Do not confuse Cannon–Bard's thalamic theory with James–Lange's peripheral bodily-feedback explanation.
