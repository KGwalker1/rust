# **Charlotte Bühler – Humanistic-Developmental Theory of Personality**

_UGC-NET JRF Psychology — Short Revision Notes_

## **1. Introduction**

- **Charlotte Malachowski Bühler (1893–1974):** Austrian-American psychologist and one of the founders of humanistic psychology.
- PhD, University of Munich (1918).
- Established the **Viennese School of Developmental Psychology** with her husband, Karl Bühler.
- Emigrated to the USA in 1939.
- President of the Association for Humanistic Psychology (1965–1966).
- Important works:
  - _Das Seelenleben des Jugendlichen_ (1922).
  - _Der menschliche Lebenslauf als psychologisches Problem_ (1933).
  - _The Course of Human Life_ (1968, with Massarik).

**Key identity:** Humanistic-developmental theorist who combined life-span development with intentionality, values and self-determination.

## **2. Core Philosophy – Intentionality and the Integrating Self**

- **Intentionality:** Human beings are goal-directed and live in pursuit of meaningful goals.
- **Integrating self:** The core system that organizes and directs the realization of goals throughout life.
- **Life as a goal-directed process:** Human development involves realizing one's best potential and achieving fulfilment.
- **Values:** Bühler emphasized the primary importance of values in human life, alongside Maslow.

**NET focus:** Intentionality + integrating self + life-span goal realization.

## **3. Four Basic Tendencies of Life (1959)**

Bühler proposed four fundamental tendencies that operate throughout life, although their prominence varies by age.

| Tendency                        | Meaning                                                | Dominant period             |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------- |
| **1. Need Satisfaction**        | Biological and social needs, sexuality and recognition | Infancy and early childhood |
| **2. Adaptive Self-Limitation** | Adjusting oneself to environmental conditions          | School age                  |
| **3. Creative Expansion**       | Innovation, self-expression and achievement            | Youth and adulthood         |
| **4. Maintaining Inner Order**  | Principles, inner harmony and self-identity            | Old age                     |

### **Consummation**

- The final goal is **consummation**: awareness of achievement and fulfilment at the end of life.
- It involves recognizing that one has lived fully, including both happiness and pain.

## **4. Five Phases of the Life Course**

Bühler's five-phase model is organized around the development of **self-determination**.

| Phase   | Age          | Characteristics                                                                         |
| ------- | ------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **I**   | Up to 16–20  | No self-determination; preparation and development of self-awareness                    |
| **II**  | Up to 25–30  | Diffuse self-determination; experimentation with careers and relationships              |
| **III** | 25–45        | Specific and definite self-determination; career, marriage and family establishment     |
| **IV**  | 45–65        | Assessment phase; evaluates successes and failures; prepares the life's “balance sheet” |
| **V**   | 65–70 onward | Old age; memories, retirement, biological decline and fulfilment or unfulfilment        |

**Important distinctions:**

- **Phase III:** Definite self-determination.
- **Phase IV:** Assessment / balance sheet of life.
- **Phase V:** Old age and life evaluation.

## **5. Three Life Realms**

Life goals are realized through three major domains:

| Realm                      | Meaning                                            |
| -------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| **Activity**               | Work, vocation, achievement and productivity       |
| **Personal Relationships** | Love, friendship and intimacy                      |
| **Self-Development**       | Self-identity, self-confidence and personal growth |

## **6. Comparison with Other Theorists**

| Feature       | Bühler                            | Maslow                        | Rogers                            | Erikson                       |
| ------------- | --------------------------------- | ----------------------------- | --------------------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| Approach      | Humanistic-developmental          | Humanistic                    | Humanistic                        | Psychoanalytic-developmental  |
| Core concept  | Intentionality, integrating self  | Hierarchy of needs            | Self-concept, congruence, UPR     | Psychosocial crises           |
| Development   | Five life phases                  | Need hierarchy                | No fixed stage model              | Eight psychosocial stages     |
| Main emphasis | Life goals and self-determination | Growth and self-actualization | Self and therapeutic relationship | Social crises across lifespan |

**NET distinction:** Bühler's stages focus on **self-determination and life goals**, whereas Erikson's stages focus on **psychosocial crises**.

## **7. Contributions**

1. Helped establish humanistic psychology alongside Maslow and Rogers.
2. Pioneered life-span developmental psychology.
3. Introduced the integrating self and emphasized intentionality.
4. Highlighted the importance of values in personality.
5. Recognized old age as an important developmental phase.

## **8. Criticisms**

1. Limited empirical validation; the model relied substantially on biographical analysis.
2. Reflects mid-20th-century Western assumptions about marriage, career and retirement.
3. Implies a relatively normative life course.
4. Descriptive rather than explanatory; mechanisms of transition are not clearly specified.
5. Overshadowed by the more widely recognized work of Maslow and Rogers.
