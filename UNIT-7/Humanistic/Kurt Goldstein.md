# **Kurt Goldstein – Organismic Theory of Personality**

_UGC-NET JRF Psychology — Short Revision Notes_

## **1. Introduction**

- **Kurt Goldstein (1878–1965):** German-American neurologist and psychiatrist; a bridge between neurology, Gestalt psychology and humanistic psychology.
- Studied **brain-injured soldiers during World War I**.
- Developed **Organismic Theory / Holistic Theory**.
- **Coined the term self-actualization (1939)**; later popularized by Maslow and Rogers.
- Major work: _The Organism: A Holistic Approach to Biology Derived from Pathological Data in Man_ (1934/1939).
- Influenced **Maslow, Rogers and Allport**.

## **2. Organismic Holism**

- The individual must be understood as a **unified whole**, not as separate psychological or physiological parts.
- Psychological and physiological events must be understood in relation to the **total organism**.
- Opposed atomistic explanations based on isolated reflexes and stimulus-response connections.
- Normal personality: unity, consistency and organization.
- Pathological personality: fragmentation and disorganization.
- Favoured an **idiographic approach**—intensive study of the individual rather than only statistical group analysis.
- The organism continuously interacts with and attempts to master its environment.

## **3. Self-Actualization: The Single Master Motive**

**Definition:** The organism's innate tendency to realize its potential and become what it is capable of becoming.

Key propositions:

1. **Sole motive:** All drives (hunger, sex, power and achievement) are expressions of self-actualization.
2. Each individual develops a unique pattern of drives according to their potential and environment.
3. Healthy functioning involves growth and actualization rather than mere self-preservation.
4. Self-actualization is a **continuous organizing process**, not a final developmental endpoint.

### **Goldstein vs. Maslow**

| Goldstein                            | Maslow                                        |
| ------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------- |
| Originated the term (1939)           | Popularized and elaborated it                 |
| Sole, universal motive               | Highest need in a hierarchy                   |
| Non-teleological, continuous process | Teleological, goal-oriented fulfillment       |
| Studied brain-injured patients       | Studied healthy, self-actualizing individuals |

## **4. Principle of Equalization**

- The organism tends to maintain an **average, balanced state** when disturbed.
- Helps maintain order and consistency despite environmental disturbances.
- Examples: turning towards a sound, cooling oneself when hot, talking to release tension.
- Enables the organism to adjust to or master environmental demands.

**Important distinction:**

- **Equalization:** Goldstein's tendency toward balance.
- **Homeostasis:** Cannon's concept of physiological equilibrium.
- Goldstein considered homeostasis insufficient to explain living organisms because they strive for growth and actualization, not merely balance.

## **5. Catastrophic Reaction and Brain Injury**

### **Catastrophic Reaction**

- Occurs when brain-injured individuals face tasks they cannot perform.
- Characterized by **anxiety, agitation and disorganization**.
- Represents the organism's inability to cope with immediate environmental demands.
- Patients may avoid difficult situations or develop compensatory behaviours to prevent the reaction.

### **Abstract vs. Concrete Attitude**

| Abstract attitude                                 | Concrete attitude                   |
| ------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| Ability to plan and anticipate                    | Immediate, stimulus-bound response  |
| Understands the essential features of a situation | Responds to the immediate situation |
| Impaired by brain injury                          | Relatively immediate and specific   |

\- Brain injury may impair abstract attitude, reducing the ability to plan and anticipate, thereby producing anxiety.

## **6. Comparison with Maslow and Rogers**

| Feature             | Goldstein                           | Maslow                        | Rogers                 |
| ------------------- | ----------------------------------- | ----------------------------- | ---------------------- |
| Key concept         | Self-actualization                  | Hierarchy of needs            | Actualizing tendency   |
| Nature              | Sole universal motive               | Highest need                  | Directional life force |
| Basis               | Brain-injured patients              | Healthy, creative individuals | Psychotherapy          |
| Approach            | Holistic, idiographic               | Idiographic                   | Phenomenological       |
| Additional concepts | Equalization, catastrophic reaction | Peak experiences              | UPR, congruence        |

## **7. Contributions**

1. Coined **self-actualization**, later central to humanistic psychology.
2. Established organismic and holistic personality theory.
3. Connected clinical neurology with personality psychology.
4. Influenced **Maslow, Rogers and Allport**.
5. Emphasized growth and actualization beyond mere homeostasis.

## **8. Criticisms**

1. Self-actualization is difficult to operationalize and experimentally test.
2. Overemphasizes inner growth and maturation.
3. Generalization from brain-injured patients to normal personality is questioned.
4. The claim that all motivation comes from one motive is difficult to establish.
5. Limited systematic empirical research.
