### 🎯 Loewenstein: Information-Gap Theory of Curiosity

**1. Core Idea**

- **Definition**: George Loewenstein (1994) proposed that curiosity is a form of **cognitively induced deprivation** that arises when an individual perceives a **gap in their knowledge or understanding** .
- **Key Insight**: Curiosity is not a positive, appetitive state in this theory—it is an **aversive feeling of deprivation** that motivates information-seeking to eliminate the gap. The theory was designed to address shortcomings in earlier curiosity theories, particularly why curiosity is intense, transitory, and often disappoints when satisfied .

**2. The Mechanism**

- **Reference Point**: Curiosity depends on a **reference-point phenomenon**. The individual must have some knowledge about a topic (a "known unknown")—if they know nothing at all, no gap is perceived, and no curiosity arises .
- **Inverted-U Relationship**: The intensity of curiosity is greatest when the gap is **moderate**. If the gap is too small (easily filled), curiosity is weak. If the gap is too large (seemingly impossible to fill), curiosity is also weak .
- **Deprivation Model**: The perceived gap produces discomfort, and the person is motivated to obtain the missing information to **reduce or eliminate** this feeling .

**3. Key Concepts for NET**

| Concept                             | Meaning                                                                                |
| :---------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Information Gap**                 | The perceived discrepancy between what one knows and what one wants to know            |
| **Cognitively Induced Deprivation** | Curiosity as an aversive state, not a pleasant one                                     |
| **Reference Point**                 | Some prior knowledge is necessary; curiosity requires a foundation to perceive the gap |
| **Moderate Gap Optimal**            | Too little or too much missing information reduces curiosity                           |

**4. Applications & Extensions**

- **Educational Context**: The theory is widely used to measure curiosity in children and design learning environments that present **manageable gaps** in knowledge to trigger curiosity .
- **Behavioral Interventions**: Researchers have used "curiosity gaps" to steer people toward desirable behaviors—for example, promising to reveal the answer to a trivia question if the person chooses the stairs over the elevator .

**5. Criticisms (Quick Points)**

- **Positive Curiosity Problem**: The theory struggles to explain instances where curiosity feels **pleasant and appetitive**, not just like deprivation. Loewenstein acknowledged this but emphasized the deprivation component .
- **Individual Differences**: The theory does not fully account for why some people are more curious than others, or how personality factors influence gap perception.
- **Overlap with Other Theories**: Some researchers note similarities with Berlyne's earlier ideas about optimal arousal, though Loewenstein explicitly did not discuss the inverted-U in his original formulation .

**Exam Tip**: Remember **Loewenstein = Information-Gap = Cognitive Deprivation**. Link the **reference point** (you must know something to feel the gap) to the **moderate gap** principle (the "known unknown" is maximally curiosity-inducing). Distinguish it from Berlyne: Berlyne focused on **arousal and collative variables**, while Loewenstein focused on **knowledge deprivation and gap-closing**.
