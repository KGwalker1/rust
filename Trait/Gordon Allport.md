# **Gordon Allport’s Trait Theory of Personality**

**UGC-NET JRF Psychology — Short Revision Notes**

## **1. Introduction**

- **Gordon Willard Allport (1897–1967):** One of the founders of personality psychology.
- Worked mainly at Harvard University.
- APA President in 1939.
- Emphasised the uniqueness of individuals through an **idiographic approach**.

**Important works:**

- _Personality: A Psychological Interpretation_ (1937).
- _The Nature of Prejudice_ (1954).
- _Pattern and Growth in Personality_ (1961).

**Important distinction:** Allport used lexical analysis and clinical observation, not factor analysis like Cattell and Eysenck.

## **2. Definition of Personality**

Allport's definition (1937):

> Personality is the dynamic organization within the individual of those psychophysical systems that determine his unique adjustments to his environment.

Later revision (1961):

> Personality is the dynamic organization within the individual of those psychophysical systems that determine his characteristic behavior and thought.

**Key terms:**

- **Dynamic organization:** Personality is active and continuously developing.
- **Psychophysical systems:** Both psychological and physical aspects.
- **Determine:** Personality influences behaviour.
- **Unique:** Every individual has a distinctive personality.

## **3. Idiographic vs. Nomothetic Approach**

Important NET distinction

| Idiographic — Allport      | Nomothetic — Cattell/Eysenck |
| -------------------------- | ---------------------------- |
| Studies unique individuals | Studies groups               |
| Personal dispositions      | Common dimensions            |
| Clinical and qualitative   | Statistical and quantitative |
| Individual uniqueness      | General personality patterns |

Allport believed personality should be understood through the study of the **unique individual**, rather than reducing everyone to a few common dimensions.

## **4. Three Levels of Traits**

Very high-yield

### **1. Cardinal Traits**

- Rare and extremely dominant.
- Shape almost the entire life and behaviour of an individual.
- Usually **1–2** in a person.
- Example: extreme ambition or altruism.

### **2. Central Traits**

- Basic characteristics used to describe a person.
- Usually **5–10** per individual.
- Examples: honesty, kindness, anxiety and friendliness.

### **3. Secondary Traits**

- Less consistent and situation-dependent.
- Appear in specific circumstances.
- Examples: particular likes, dislikes and preferences.
- Can be numerous or infinite.

| Trait     | Main feature          | Number   |
| --------- | --------------------- | -------- |
| Cardinal  | Dominant, rare        | 1–2      |
| Central   | Basic characteristics | 5–10     |
| Secondary | Situation-specific    | Infinite |

## **5. Common Traits vs. Personal Dispositions**

**Common Traits**

- Shared by many people within a culture.
- Allow comparison between individuals.
- Useful in group-based research.

**Personal Dispositions**

- Individual characteristics unique to a person.
- Allport's preferred concept.
- Represent a person's distinctive patterns of behaviour.

**NET trap:** Allport used the term **personal dispositions** for individual-specific traits.

## **6. Proprium — Core Self**

- Allport used the term **Proprium** instead of self or ego.
- Refers to the core aspects of personality that create a sense of inner unity.
- Includes body sense, personal identity, self-esteem, values, attitudes and intentions.
- Develops gradually.

**Important:** Inner unity does not necessarily mean complete harmony.

Do not confuse:

- Allport → Proprium.
- Rogers → Self-concept.
- Jung → Self archetype.
- Freud → Ego.

## **7. Functional Autonomy of Motives**

Signature concept

**Core idea:** Adult motives can become independent of the original motives that produced them.

Example: A person initially starts running for health but continues because running itself becomes enjoyable.

### **Two Types**

| Type                                  | Meaning                                        | Example                                   |
| ------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| **Perseverative functional autonomy** | Repetitive habits continue independently       | Brushing teeth                            |
| **Propriate functional autonomy**     | Higher-level motives connected to the proprium | Pursuing a career for personal fulfilment |

**Significance:**

- Adult behaviour need not be explained entirely by childhood experiences.
- Motives operate in the present, even if they originated in the past.

**Criticism:**

- McClelland (1942) criticised functional autonomy as difficult to establish.
- Oppenheimer (1958) argued that apparently autonomous motives may be secondary motives derived from primary ones.

## **8. Four Requirements of Motivation**

Allport proposed four requirements for an adequate theory of motivation:

1. **Contemporaneity:** Motives operate in the present.
2. **Pluralistic:** Motivation has multiple sources; it cannot be reduced to one principle.
3. **Cognitive process:** Motivation involves thinking and planning.
4. **Concrete uniqueness:** Motivation should be studied in individual cases.

**NET point:** Contemporaneity is directly related to functional autonomy.

## **9. Allport–Vernon–Lindzey Study of Values**

Developed from **Eduard Spranger's** six value orientations.

| Value       | Meaning                           |
| ----------- | --------------------------------- |
| Theoretical | Search for truth and knowledge    |
| Economic    | Practicality and usefulness       |
| Aesthetic   | Beauty and artistic experience    |
| Social      | Love and concern for people       |
| Political   | Power and influence               |
| Religious   | Unity and spiritual understanding |

**Remember:** Allport, Vernon and Lindzey developed the Study of Values based on Spranger's classification.

## **10. Expressive vs. Coping Behaviour**

| Coping Behaviour        | Expressive Behaviour   |
| ----------------------- | ---------------------- |
| Goal-directed           | Spontaneous            |
| The "what" of behaviour | The "how" of behaviour |
| Problem-solving         | Reveals personal style |
| Adaptive                | Personality-revealing  |

Example: Repairing a lock is coping behaviour; the particular manner in which someone repairs it is expressive behaviour.

**NET point:** Expressive behaviour reveals personality more directly.

## **11. Traits and Situational Consistency**

- Allport believed traits are real psychological structures.
- However, behaviour may vary across situations.
- Different situations activate different traits to different degrees.
- He recognised the influence of both the **person and situation**.
- He did not develop a complete interactionist process theory.

**Important:** Allport did not assume that traits always produce identical behaviour across every situation.

## **12. Comparison with Other Trait Theorists**

| Theorist           | Main concept                                        | Method               |
| ------------------ | --------------------------------------------------- | -------------------- |
| **Allport**        | Cardinal, central, secondary; personal dispositions | Idiographic, lexical |
| **Cattell**        | 16 PF; surface and source traits                    | Factor analysis      |
| **Eysenck**        | PEN model                                           | Factor analysis      |
| **McCrae & Costa** | Big Five (OCEAN)                                    | Factor analysis      |

## **13. Contributions**

1. Established personality psychology as a systematic field of study.
2. Developed the cardinal, central and secondary trait hierarchy.
3. Introduced functional autonomy of motives.
4. Promoted idiographic study of individuals.
5. Developed the concept of proprium.
6. Contributed significantly to prejudice research through _The Nature of Prejudice_ (1954).

## **14. Criticisms**

1. Functional autonomy is difficult to prove empirically.
2. Idiographic research has limitations in generalisation and replication.
3. Trait hierarchy is more descriptive than explanatory.
4. Gives limited attention to unconscious processes.
5. Does not provide a comprehensive developmental stage theory.
6. Lacks a large empirical research programme compared with Cattell and Eysenck.

## **16. Common NET MCQ Traps**

- **Cardinal → Central → Secondary**: Allport's trait hierarchy.
- **Personal dispositions**: Individual-specific traits.
- **Proprium**: Allport's core self.
- **Functional autonomy**: Adult motives become independent of original motives.
- **Perseverative autonomy**: Repetitive habits.
- **Propriate autonomy**: Higher-level motives connected to the proprium.
- **Contemporaneity**: Motives operate in the present.
- **Expressive behaviour**: Reveals personality.
- **Spranger**: Six value orientations.
- **Cattell**: Factor analysis and 16 PF, not Allport.
